There is a coffee shop on the ground floor of this building.

Between 9:00 and 9:20 on a teaching morning it serves about thirty people, on
two members of staff, and for most of those twenty minutes the line is out of
the door.

<Beat n={1} label="Ask the wrong question first">

Most people, asked why that line exists, answer *not enough staff*.

Hold on to that answer. It is the one this entire course is built to
complicate, and by week 4 you will be able to say precisely what is wrong with
it. For now, notice only that it was reached in under a second, by everybody,
without looking at anything.

Here is a better question, and it is not rhetorical:

<Punch>Why is the line *that shape*?</Punch>

Not why there is a line. Why it runs along the window rather than doubling
back. Why there is one queue and not two. Why the person taking your order is
also the person making the drink. Why somebody who wants a filter coffee waits
behind somebody ordering four oat lattes.

Each of those is a decision. Not one of them was made by the people standing in
the line, and most of them were not made by the people behind the counter
either. They arrived with the fit-out, or with a franchise manual, or with a
staff rota written to cover lunch.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Look at what the room is actually doing">

Below is the same café three ways: same floor area, same two staff, same
customers arriving at the same rate. The only thing the selector changes is how
the room is organised. Run each one through the morning rush and watch where the
line ends up.

None of these is the natural shape of a queue, because <Key>there is no natural
shape of a queue</Key>. Each is a decision. Some of them were made deliberately by
somebody who had thought about throughput. At least one was made by a
shopfitter in 2019 who was thinking about where the power outlets go.

</Beat>

<Predict question="One of those three is costing about four minutes per customer at peak. Which, and why?">

The first — one line, one person doing both jobs. At a rush of a customer and a
half a minute it costs about **4.7 minutes** each. The third arrangement, on the
same two staff, costs **0.8**.

Not because anybody is working harder. Taking an order is ten seconds and making
the drink is twenty-five, so one person doing both spends thirty-five seconds a
customer and the machine stands idle for ten of them. Split the two jobs across
the same two people and the slow one never stops: the room's capacity is now set
by the drink alone.

Then push the rush past two a minute and something awkward happens. The *third*
arrangement runs out of capacity before the *second* does, because two
specialists in series can only go as fast as the slower of them, while two
generalists share all the work. Two lines are worse at the rush the café
actually gets and better at the one it does not.

Which is the real lesson, and it is not "this layout is best": each of these
rooms is optimal for a different morning, and nobody in that building could tell
you which morning they chose, or when, or why.

</Predict>

<Beat n={3} label="Four parts, and only four">

Strip any queue anywhere down and you find the same four parts. Almost
everything in the next twelve weeks is a consequence of turning one of them.

**Arrivals — when people turn up.** In the café this is about ninety people an
hour at peak, and the important thing about that number is not its size but its
*raggedness*: a tutorial ends and eleven people arrive in forty seconds. Week 2
is about nothing else.

**Service — how long each one takes.** Ten seconds to take the order,
twenty-five to make the drink, and a flat white is not a round of four. The
spread matters more than the average, which is the other half of week 2.

**Servers — how many at once.** Two. It is the knob everyone reaches for first,
it is the only one that costs money, and week 5 shows that *how you arrange* two
servers changes the wait more than going from two to three does.

**Discipline — who goes next.** The café's answer is "whoever has been in the
line longest", except when it is "whoever is holding the right card", or
"whoever the barista makes eye contact with", or "whoever ordered something the
machine is already making".

The fourth one is the one nobody notices and everybody has opinions about. No
one has ever been angry about inter-arrival variance. Everyone has been angry
about queue-jumping.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="Queues you would not call queues">

Before we go further, widen the word, because the habit this course is trying
to build is worth very little if it only fires in a coffee shop.

A queue is any place where **work arrives faster than it can be done, and
something has to decide the order**. That is the whole definition. It does not
require a floor, a rope, or a person standing on their feet.

- The **lift lobby** at 8:55. Nobody thinks of it as a line, everybody behaves
  exactly as if it were one, and the lift's scheduling algorithm is making a
  who-goes-first decision several times a second.
- The **print spooler**, which is a queue with a discipline you could look up in
  a manual and almost certainly never have.
- **Hold music.** Week 11 is about what happens when that queue can talk back to
  the people in it.
- A **housing waiting list**, which is a queue measured in years and sorted by a
  points system somebody wrote down and published.
- The **organ transplant list**, where the ordering rule was argued about in
  public for two decades because everyone understood what it decided. That is
  week 12, and it is where the course ends.
- The **retry** your browser just did, silently, against a server that was busy.

Each of those has arrivals, service, servers and a discipline. Several of them
have a discipline that has never been written down. <Key>The invisible ones are
where the interesting failures live</Key>, because a rule nobody has stated is a
rule nobody has argued with.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="Where the mathematics came from, and who it was for">

Worth knowing, because it explains the shape of the whole field.

In 1909, a Danish mathematician called **Agner Krarup Erlang**, working for the
Copenhagen Telephone Company, published a paper on the theory of probabilities
applied to telephone conversations. The company had a practical problem: how
many lines does an exchange need so that callers are not turned away, given that
callers arrive at random and talk for random lengths of time?

That is the founding question of queueing theory, and notice what it is *not*
about. It is not about fairness, or whose call matters more, or who is standing
in the rain. It is a capacity-planning question asked by an operator about a
machine. The unit of offered traffic is still called the **erlang**, and the
formulas he derived for how often a caller finds every line busy are still
called Erlang B and Erlang C, and are still in use in every call centre in the
world.

<Punch note="Which is why the standard toolkit is superb at how long and nearly silent about who — it was built by operators, for operators, about equipment.">
  The mathematics of waiting was invented to size telephone exchanges, not to
  arbitrate between people.
</Punch>

The orderly single-file line is younger than you would guess, and it is a social
technology rather than a natural one. Crowds at markets and ticket windows for
most of recorded history were a press, not a line. Queueing as a *norm* — the
idea that the correct way to distribute a scarce thing is to stand behind the
person who arrived before you, and that jumping is a moral offence — is
generally traced to the industrial era, and in Britain it hardened into a
national self-image during wartime rationing.

Which is to say: the rule you are about to spend four weeks taking apart was
itself invented, quite recently, by people who had to be persuaded of it.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="The claim this course makes">

> A queue is not a thing that happens to people. It is
> <Key>a rule, applied to people, by someone, usually invisibly</Key>.

Deciding <Key>who goes first is deciding whose time gets spent</Key>. Time is what
everybody in that line is paying with, whether they agreed to or not.

So the rule is moving cost from one group of people to another — and it is
almost never saying so, to people who have no way to bill anyone for the hours.

<Punch note="It will come back in week 4 with numbers attached, in week 8 with a price attached, and in week 12 attached to a transplant waiting list.">
  There is no neutral queue.
</Punch>

This is not a queueing theory course, though you will have the core of the
mathematics by week 3 and it takes about forty minutes. The mathematics answers
*how long*. It does not answer *who*.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} label="The habit to build">

<Key>**Name the rule before you judge it.**</Key>

A remarkable amount of complaint about waiting turns out, on inspection, to be
complaint about a rule the complainer has never articulated. Once you can state
it — as an instruction, beginning *"when a server becomes free, serve…"* — you
can ask the question that actually goes somewhere: what would it cost to change?

The café above states its own. The sentence along the bottom of the frame is
that arrangement's rule written out as an instruction, and the figure in pink
is the person the instruction has already picked; switch the room and watch
both of them change.

Yours will be harder than that, because the queue you choose will not come with
a selector. Three things go wrong, and all three are the point of this week's
lab:

**You will write "first come, first served" and be wrong.** Look again for
thirty seconds and you will usually find a second rule running underneath: an
app order that skips the line, a regular who gets served out of turn, a till
that only takes cards, a member of staff who serves whoever is closest.

**You will describe the intention, not the behaviour.** The instruction has to
be one a new starter could follow on their first shift without asking anybody
what it means.

**You will find nobody in the building knows.** This is normal and it is the
most interesting result you can get. A rule that has never been stated has never
been defended, which means it has never been compared with the alternative.

</Beat>

<Beat n={8} label="How the next twelve weeks go">

Every week runs the same shape, and it is deliberately not
definition-then-explanation:

**observation → model → experiment → consequence → design decision.**

You will be shown a real place first, given the smallest model that explains it,
handed the controls, and then asked what the result costs and who it lands on.

The semester is in three acts.

**Act I — why waiting happens** (weeks 1–4). The physics. You will learn to see
a queue, explain why it forms when nobody is overloaded, measure one from
outside with no cooperation from anybody, and put a price on the result.

**Act II — who goes first** (weeks 5–9). The rules. Five different ways to order
the same people — arrival, length, need, price, appointment — and for each one,
what it buys, and who pays for it.

**Act III — systems that fight back** (weeks 10–12). The complications. Queues
joined to other queues, queues full of people who have worked out what you are
doing, and finally a queue where the ordering rule had to be argued about in
public because everyone could see what it decided.

Four pieces of assessment sit along that spine. You will measure a real queue
(week 3), change a real rule and name the loser (week 6), run an experiment
properly (week 9), and redesign something end to end (week 12). None of them
asks you to recall a definition.

</Beat>

<Beat n={9} label="Where this goes">

Next week, two systems. Both serve exactly sixty customers an hour. Both have
one server working at exactly the same average speed.

One of them has no queue at all. The other has eleven people in it.

**Nothing about how busy they are can explain the difference.**

</Beat>
