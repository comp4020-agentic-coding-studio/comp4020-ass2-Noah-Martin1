There is a coffee shop on the ground floor of this building.

Between 9:00 and 9:20 on a teaching morning it serves about thirty people, on
two members of staff, and for most of those twenty minutes the line is out of
the door.

<Beat n={1} label="Ask the wrong question first">

Most people, asked why that line exists, answer *not enough staff*.

Hold on to that answer. It is the one this entire course is built to
complicate, and by week 4 you will be able to say precisely what is wrong with
it. For now, notice only that it was reached in under a second, by everybody,
without looking at anything.

Here is a better question, and it is not rhetorical:

<Punch>Why is the line *that shape*?</Punch>

Not why there is a line. Why it runs along the window rather than doubling
back. Why there is one queue and not two. Why the person taking your order is
also the person making the drink. Why somebody who wants a filter coffee waits
behind somebody ordering four oat lattes.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Look at what the room is actually doing">

Below is the same café three ways: same floor area, same two staff, same
customers arriving at the same rate. The only thing the selector changes is how
the room is organised. Run each one through the morning rush and watch where the
line ends up.

None of these is the natural shape of a queue, because <Key>there is no natural
shape of a queue</Key>. Each is a decision. Some of them were made deliberately by
somebody who had thought about throughput. At least one was made by a
shopfitter in 2019 who was thinking about where the power outlets go.

</Beat>

<Predict question="One of those three is costing about four minutes per customer at peak. Which, and why?">

The first — one line, one person doing both jobs. At a rush of a customer and a
half a minute it costs about **4.7 minutes** each. The third arrangement, on the
same two staff, costs **0.8**.

Not because anybody is working harder. Taking an order is ten seconds and making
the drink is twenty-five, so one person doing both spends thirty-five seconds a
customer and the machine stands idle for ten of them. Split the two jobs across
the same two people and the slow one never stops: the room's capacity is now set
by the drink alone.

Then push the rush past two a minute and something awkward happens. The *third*
arrangement runs out of capacity before the *second* does, because two
specialists in series can only go as fast as the slower of them, while two
generalists share all the work. Two lines are worse at the rush the café
actually gets and better at the one it does not.

Which is the real lesson, and it is not "this layout is best": each of these
rooms is optimal for a different morning, and nobody in that building could tell
you which morning they chose, or when, or why.

</Predict>

<Beat n={3} label="The claim this course makes">

> A queue is not a thing that happens to people. It is
> <Key>a rule, applied to people, by someone, usually invisibly</Key>.

Every queue has four parts, and almost everything in the next twelve weeks is a
consequence of turning one of them.

- **Arrivals** — when people turn up
- **Service** — how long each one takes
- **Servers** — how many at once
- **Discipline** — *who goes next*

The fourth one is the one nobody notices and everybody has opinions about. No
one has ever been angry about inter-arrival variance. Everyone has been angry
about queue-jumping.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="Why the fourth knob is the political one">

Deciding <Key>who goes first is deciding whose time gets spent</Key>. Time is what
everybody in that line is paying with, whether they agreed to or not.

So the rule is moving cost from one group of people to another — and it is
almost never saying so, to people who have no way to bill anyone for the hours.

<Punch note="It will come back in week 4 with numbers attached, in week 8 with a price attached, and in week 12 attached to a transplant waiting list.">
  There is no neutral queue.
</Punch>

This is not a queueing theory course, though you will have the core of the
mathematics by week 3 and it takes about forty minutes. The mathematics answers
*how long*. It does not answer *who*.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="The habit to build">

<Key>**Name the rule before you judge it.**</Key>

A remarkable amount of complaint about waiting turns out, on inspection, to be
complaint about a rule the complainer has never articulated. Once you can state
it — as an instruction, beginning *"when a server becomes free, serve…"* — you
can ask the question that actually goes somewhere: what would it cost to change?

That instruction is your lab exercise this week, and you will find it harder to
write than it looks.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Where this goes">

Next week, two systems. Both serve exactly sixty customers an hour. Both have
one server working at exactly the same average speed.

One of them has no queue at all. The other has eleven people in it.

**Nothing about how busy they are can explain the difference.**

</Beat>
