There is a coffee shop on the ground floor of this building. Between 9:00 and
9:20 on a teaching morning it serves roughly ninety people, and for most of that
twenty minutes there is a line out the door.

Here is the question the course opens with, and it is not rhetorical: **why is
the line in that shape?**

Not "why is there a line" — that one is easy, more people want coffee than the
machine can produce at once. The question is why the line runs *along the
window* rather than doubling back, why there is one queue rather than two, why
the person taking orders is not the person making the drinks, and why somebody
who orders a filter coffee waits behind somebody ordering four oat lattes
instead of being handed their cup in fifteen seconds.

None of those are laws of physics. Each one is a decision. Some of them were
made deliberately by somebody who had thought about throughput. Some were made
by a shopfitter in 2019 who was thinking about power outlets. At least one of
them is costing about four minutes per customer at peak, and nobody in the
building could tell you which.

## The claim this course makes

> A queue is not a thing that happens to people. It is a rule, applied to
> people, by someone, usually invisibly.

That rule decides who goes first. Deciding who goes first is deciding whose time
is spent, and time is the one resource every person in the line is paying with
whether they agreed to or not. So the rule is doing distributive work — it is
moving cost from one group of people to another — and it is almost always doing
it without saying so.

This is what I mean by the economics of waiting, and it is why the course is not
simply a queueing theory unit. The mathematics is a tool, and a good one; we
will have the core of it by week 3. But the mathematics answers *how long*. It
does not answer *who*, and *who* is the interesting question.

## What we do today

Three things, in order.

First, a taxonomy you can use immediately: every queue has an **arrival
process**, a **service process**, a **number of servers**, and a **discipline** —
the rule for choosing who is served next. Four knobs. Almost everything in this
course is a consequence of turning one of them.

Second, the observation that the discipline is the knob people notice least and
argue about most, once they notice it. Nobody has strong feelings about
inter-arrival variance. Everybody has strong feelings about queue-jumping.

Third, the course's running commitment, which I will hold you to in the final
brief: **name the rule before you judge it.** A remarkable amount of complaint
about waiting turns out, on inspection, to be complaint about a rule the
complainer has never articulated. Once you can state it, you can ask the useful
question, which is what it would cost to change.

## Where this goes

Next week we take the four knobs and turn the first one. It will turn out that
the thing that makes queues unbearable is not how busy a system is — plenty of
very busy systems have no queue at all — but how *unpredictable* it is. That is
a more counter-intuitive result than it sounds, and it explains why adding one
more barista sometimes fixes nothing.

Your lab this week is the ninety seconds of looking that most people never do.
