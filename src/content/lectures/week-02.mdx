<Beat n={2} label="Why this is not obvious">

Everyone's instinct is that a queue forms when a system runs out of capacity.
It does not. A perfectly predictable system running at 99% of capacity has no
queue at all — you can verify that yourself in the simulation below by dragging
variability to zero and load to 0.97, and watching the line empty.

What creates a queue is <Key>a gap: a moment when work arrives and the server is
still busy with something that ran long</Key>. Regularity closes those gaps.
Irregularity opens them, and once open they do not close by themselves, because
the server can only ever work at one speed.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="The trap in the last few per cent">

Define **ρ** — "rho" — as the fraction of capacity you are using: arrival rate
divided by service rate.

<Predict question="As ρ goes from 0.7 to 0.9, how much worse does waiting get?">

About **three times** worse. Then from 0.9 to 0.95 it roughly doubles again.

Almost nobody guesses this. The intuition is that going from 70% to 90% busy
makes things maybe 30% worse, because that is what linear feels like.

Queue length grows roughly with <Key>**ρ/(1−ρ)**</Key>, and that denominator is the whole
story: as ρ approaches 1 it approaches zero, and the ratio runs away. The last
few per cent of capacity you squeeze out of a system are paid for with an
enormous quantity of somebody else's time.

</Predict>

Drive the dial yourself. Start at 0.8 and creep upward.

<Beat n={4} label="What this means for anyone running anything">

A system deliberately run at 85% capacity is not a wasteful system. <Key>It has
bought predictability with slack</Key>, and the slack is doing real work.

When a hospital ward, a motorway or a support desk is pushed to 97%
"efficiency", the efficiency is real — and so is the collapse in service. The
second is rarely attributed to the first, because the utilisation figure went
in the right direction and utilisation is what gets reported.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="Where variability comes from">

Two sources, worth keeping apart because you can do different things about them.

**Arrival variability** is people turning up when they want. You mostly cannot
remove it. You can sometimes reshape it, which is week 9's entire subject.

**Service variability** is transactions taking different lengths of time. You
can often reduce it, and reducing it is frequently cheaper than adding
capacity. The drive-through that splits ordering from payment from collection
has not added a server — it has cut the variance each station sees. So has the
café in week 1's third floor plan.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Hold on to this aircraft">
