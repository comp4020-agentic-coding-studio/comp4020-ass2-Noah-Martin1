Two systems. Both serve exactly sixty customers an hour. Both have exactly one
server, working at exactly the same average speed.

One of them has no queue, ever. The other has a queue of eleven people.

The difference is not effort, staffing, or management. It is that in the first
system the customers arrive one per minute like clockwork and every transaction
takes fifty-five seconds. In the second, customers arrive whenever they feel
like it and some of them order for the whole office.

This is the single most useful idea in the first half of the course, and it is
routinely missed by the people running real systems: **queues are made of
variability, not of load.** A perfectly predictable system running at 99% of
capacity has no queue. A wildly unpredictable one at 70% can have a permanent
one.

## The utilisation trap

Define ρ — "rho" — as the fraction of capacity you are using: arrival rate
divided by service rate. Everyone's intuition is that as ρ climbs from 0.7 to
0.9, waiting gets somewhat worse. Roughly 30% worse, say.

It gets about **three times** worse. From 0.9 to 0.95, it doubles again.

The relationship is not linear and it is not close to linear. Queue length grows
roughly with ρ/(1−ρ), which means the last few per cent of capacity you squeeze
out of a system are paid for with an enormous amount of somebody else's time.
Run the simulation below and watch it happen; the dial is the only control, and
the thing to notice is how ordinary the queue looks at 0.8 and how fast it
stops looking ordinary after that.

This has a direct managerial consequence that almost nobody acts on. A system
deliberately run at 85% capacity is not a wasteful system. It is a system that
has bought predictability with slack, and the slack is doing real work. When a
hospital ward, a motorway or a support desk is pushed to 97% "efficiency", the
efficiency is real and so is the collapse in service, and the second is not
usually attributed to the first.

## Where the variability comes from

Two places, and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Arrival variability** is people turning up when they want. You mostly cannot
remove it; you can sometimes reshape it, which is week 9's whole subject.

**Service variability** is transactions taking different lengths of time. You
can often reduce it, and reducing it is frequently cheaper than adding
capacity — the drive-through that splits ordering from payment from collection
has not added a server, it has cut the variance each station sees.

## A system to keep in mind

Hold on to this: an aircraft boarding through a single door is a queue with
brutal service variability. Every passenger who stops to load a bag into an
overhead locker blocks everyone behind them for an unpredictable interval. We
come back to that aircraft in week 10, with three more tools than we have today,
and it turns out to be the best worked example in the course.
