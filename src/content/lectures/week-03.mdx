You are at the back of the airport security line. You cannot see the front. No
sign tells you the wait, and if one did you would be right not to believe it.

You can, however, see two things. You can count roughly how many people are
ahead of you — call it 240. And you can stand still for two minutes and count
how many people pass a fixed point, which gives you a throughput — say 40 per
minute.

Your expected wait is six minutes. You now know something the airport has not
told you, using arithmetic you did in primary school.

## The law

**L = λW.** The average number of items in a system equals the average arrival
rate multiplied by the average time each one spends there.

That is the whole thing. What makes it remarkable is not the algebra but the
generality: it holds for any queue in steady state, with any arrival
distribution, any service distribution, any number of servers, and *any
discipline whatsoever*. First come first served, shortest job first, random,
priority, corrupt — Little's Law does not care. It is one of the very few
results in this field that survives contact with a real system unmodified.

The proof is a page of accounting and we will do it properly in the lecture. The
intuition is simpler: over a long window, everyone who came in went out, so the
total person-minutes accumulated inside the system can be counted two ways —
by watching the room, or by adding up individual stays. Both counts are of the
same quantity, so they are equal.

## What it is actually good for

Three things, in rising order of usefulness.

**Estimating a wait from the outside.** As above. Useful, slightly parlour-trick.

**Catching a lie.** If a service claims an average wait of 10 minutes, and you
can see 500 people in the system and count 20 arrivals a minute, the claimed
wait is inconsistent with the observable facts by a factor of two and a half.
Someone is measuring from a different starting line than you are — often the
moment a ticket is *called* rather than the moment it is *taken*. Nearly every
published waiting-time statistic hides a definitional choice like this, and
L = λW is the cheapest tool for finding it.

**Sizing a system you have not built.** Rearranged, W = L/λ tells you how long
people will wait given a capacity, before you commit to the capacity.

## The trap

Steady state. The law describes a system where what comes in matches what goes
out over your measurement window. Point it at a lunch rush that starts empty and
ends with forty people still queueing and it will give you a confident, precise,
wrong answer.

That failure is not a footnote — it is most of the difficulty in your first
assignment, which is due at the end of this week. Measuring a real queue is
mostly the discipline of noticing when the assumptions you are relying on have
quietly stopped being true.

that costs, or who pays it. That turns out to be the harder half.
