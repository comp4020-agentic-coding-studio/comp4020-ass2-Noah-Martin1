A passport office processes 800 applications a day. The average in-person wait
is 90 minutes.

You now have the tools to do something with those two numbers that the office
itself has never done.

<Beat n={1} label="Multiply it out">

800 applications × 90 minutes is 1,200 hours of human life per day, at that one
office. Over a working year, about 300,000 hours — roughly a hundred and sixty
people's entire working lives, spent sitting in a room.

Neither number required anyone's cooperation. Last week's arithmetic produced
both of them from outside the building:

<Punch>
  Waiting is a real cost that is systematically invisible to the organisation
  generating it.
</Punch>

<Beat n={2} label="Why that changes behaviour">

<Key>An organisation optimises what it can see.</Key>

When the cost of waiting sits entirely outside its accounts, the rational
internal decision is nearly always to push the queue rather than the budget:
run at 97% utilisation, cut the counter from four windows to three, add a
verification step that costs the office thirty seconds and the queue forty
minutes.

That middle one is the control at the top of this page, and it is worth doing
by hand. At fourteen applications an hour, going from four open windows to
three takes the average wait from sixteen minutes to forty-eight and the day's
waiting from 28 person-hours to 86. <Key>The office saves $304 a day and the
room loses $2,303</Key> — seven and a half dollars of somebody else's time for
every dollar saved, and not one line of it in the office's ledger.

Watch the mechanism rather than the numbers, because it generalises past
passport offices. Three windows instead of four is a small change in ρ and a
large change in ρ/(1−ρ) — week 2's table, with a public counter on it. The
manager did not make a large decision. **The curve made it large for them**, and
the curve was not on the report.

None of these are failures of management. Each is a correct decision given the
information the manager is scored on. The failure is upstream, in what gets
measured — which is why *"the staff are lazy"* is almost always the wrong
diagnosis of a long queue, and why week 1's instinctive answer, *not enough
staff*, is the wrong one too.

The general form is worth naming, because you will meet it in every remaining
week of this course. **An externality is a cost borne by somebody outside the
transaction that produced it.** Pollution is the textbook case. Waiting is the
same structure, paid in hours instead of particulates, and with one extra
feature that makes it worse: the people paying it are assembled, in a room, in
front of the organisation, and it still does not appear.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="How an hour gets priced, when anybody bothers">

It would be reasonable to think that $40 figure is made up. It is not arbitrary,
and the way it is arrived at in practice is worth knowing, because
<Key>your hour is already being priced — just not by the people making you
wait</Key>.

Transport economics does this routinely and at enormous scale. Every national
appraisal guidance for road and rail publishes standard **values of travel time
savings**: a schedule of what an hour saved is worth, per person, by journey
purpose. Those numbers decide which motorways get built. A scheme that shaves
four minutes off two hundred thousand daily journeys is justified, or not, on
exactly the arithmetic you did at the top of this page.

Two features of that practice matter here.

**The value depends on the purpose, not the person.** Business travel is
typically valued at or near what the employer is paying for that hour, on the
argument that the time is genuinely lost output. Commuting and leisure travel
are valued lower, and derived from what people actually do — how much extra they
pay for a faster route, how far they will drive for cheaper fuel — rather than
from their wage.

**The numbers are published and argued about.** Not perfect, not uncontested,
but *on the page*, where somebody can object to them.

Set that against the passport office, which has implicitly priced its queue at
zero and published nothing. The contrast is the lecture: this is not a hard
problem that nobody has solved. It is a solved problem that this particular
organisation has no reason to apply, because the hours are not its hours.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="Now the uncomfortable arithmetic">

Say we accept that waiting should be costed. At what rate?

**Price each hour at what that person would pay to avoid it.** This is the
economically orthodox answer, and it gives a defensible number. It also gives
the result that a barrister's hour is worth forty times a pensioner's — so any
system allocating on that basis will serve the barrister first, with a rigorous
efficiency argument for doing so.

**Price every hour identically.** This is what most people's instincts prefer.
It is not a measurement. <Key>It is a moral commitment wearing a spreadsheet</Key>, and it
should be defended as one rather than smuggled in as arithmetic.

**Price it at zero.** This is what the passport office is doing right now, and
it is the option that produced the 300,000 hours.

<Predict question="Which of those three is the neutral choice?">

None of them. That is the point, and it is the course's thesis arriving on
schedule.

There is no option here that avoids making a distributional decision, including
the option of not choosing — which in practice means keeping whatever rule the
system already had, and enforcing it on everyone who walks in.

By declining to price waiting, the passport office has not stayed out of the
argument. It has taken the third position, and declined to look at it.

</Predict>

There is a fourth answer, and it is the one worth carrying into Act II: **price
it differently depending on what the queue is for.** An hour lost at a passport
office and an hour lost in an emergency department are not the same object, and
a single exchange rate for time cannot express the difference. Week 7 is about a
queue where the thing being traded is not hours at all, and where that is
precisely why everybody consents to a rule they would resist anywhere else.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="The part that is not hours">

Everything so far has treated a wait as a quantity of time and nothing else.
That is a useful simplification and it is badly incomplete, because the
experience of waiting is not proportional to the clock.

The classic statement of this is David Maister's, from the mid-1980s, and it is
a short list of propositions that anyone who has run a service will recognise
immediately:

- **Unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time.** Hence magazines, mirrors
  by the lifts, and the menu board positioned where the queue can read it.
- **Waiting before a process starts feels longer than waiting during it.** Being
  seated and ignored beats standing at the door, even for the same total wait.
- **Anxiety makes waits feel longer.** *Did my form go through? Is this the
  right line?*
- **Uncertain waits feel longer than known, finite waits.** Ten minutes you were
  told about beats five minutes you were not.
- **Unexplained waits feel longer than explained ones.** "We are one staff member
  down today" is worth several minutes of actual time.
- **Unfair waits feel longer than equitable ones.** One person served out of
  turn poisons the entire line, and everybody in it can tell you exactly how
  many minutes it cost them, which is never the true figure.

<Punch note="Which is why the cheapest intervention available to almost any service is information, and why week 11 is about what happens when you give it.">
  Perceived waiting and measured waiting are different quantities, and only one
  of them is on the dashboard.
</Punch>

Take this the right way round. It is **not** a licence to leave the wait alone
and manage the feeling instead — that is a real and common abuse, and week 11
takes it apart. It is a warning that the 300,000 hours is a *lower bound* on the
damage, and that two systems with identical L, λ and W can be experienced as
completely different places.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Who is actually in the room">

Look at who is standing in that passport queue at 10am on a Tuesday. It is
disproportionately people who cannot pay to avoid it, cannot send somebody
else, cannot take the appointment at 2pm on a Wednesday, and cannot do it
online.

<Key>The queue is not a random sample of the population.</Key> Any rule you apply to it
lands on *that* population, not on an average one — and the rules that look
most neutral in the abstract are often the ones that land hardest, because
neutrality is exactly what stops them noticing who is there.

There is a sharper version of this, and it changes what the 300,000 hours means.
An hour is not equally available to everybody. A salaried worker with flexible
hours spends an hour. A shift worker on an hourly contract spends an hour *and*
the wages for it, plus possibly the shift. A single parent spends an hour and a
childcare problem. Researchers on **administrative burden** separate out three
costs that a citizen pays to obtain something they are entitled to: the
*learning* cost of finding out how, the *compliance* cost of doing it — of which
queueing is the purest form — and the *psychological* cost of being processed.

All three fall hardest on exactly the people most likely to need the service.
Which produces the result that should end any argument that a queue is
politically neutral: <Key>raising the hassle of a service reduces its take-up,
and reduces it most among the people it was designed for</Key>.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} label="When the wait is the point">

Now the uncomfortable extension, and it is not hypothetical.

Economists have a name for a barrier deliberately placed in front of a benefit
so that only those who really need it will bother: an **ordeal mechanism**. The
argument is genuinely clever. If you cannot observe who is truly in need, make
the benefit costly to obtain in a currency the needy are more willing to spend
than the non-needy — and time is the obvious candidate, because it cannot be
borrowed, transferred or counterfeited. A queue, on this reading, is a
means-test that requires no paperwork and cannot be lied to.

It sometimes works. It is also, quite often, exactly backwards.

<Predict question="A benefit office adds a two-hour in-person queue to screen out people who do not really need the payment. Who does it screen out?">

Disproportionately, the people who need it most.

The mechanism assumes the poor are time-rich and the comfortable are time-poor.
For a pensioner and a consultant that may hold. For a parent working two hourly
shifts with no leave and no childcare, it is straightforwardly false: they are
the most time-poor person in the building, and the two-hour ordeal is the
barrier most likely to defeat them.

So the screen selects on *flexibility of schedule*, which correlates with income
in a direction nobody intended. The office will report a fall in claims and,
depending on what it was measuring, may record that as a success.

<Key>An ordeal targets whoever can most afford the currency it charges, and time
is not the currency people think it is.</Key>

</Predict>

You should not leave this beat thinking every queue is a conspiracy. Most are
not. But when you find a wait that nobody has tried to shorten despite it being
cheap to shorten, the possibility that **the wait is doing a job for somebody**
belongs on your list of hypotheses. It comes back in week 8, where the job it is
doing is making a fast lane worth paying for.

</Beat>

<Beat n={8} label="What Act I gave you">

Four weeks, four tools, and they compose.

**Week 1.** A queue is a designed object with four parts, and the fourth one —
who goes next — is a rule somebody chose. *Name the rule before you judge it.*

**Week 2.** Queues are made of variability, not load, and the cost of load is
non-linear: W ≈ V × U × T. *Look at the spread before you look at the average.*

**Week 3.** L = λW lets you measure any queue from outside, and the boundary you
draw around it is the claim you are making. *State the box before you state the
number.*

**Week 4.** The wait has a price, the organisation causing it cannot see that
price, and the people paying it are not a random sample. *Find out who is
holding the bill.*

Put them together and you can walk into a building, watch a line for ninety
seconds, say why it is there, estimate what it costs, and name who is paying.
That is the toolkit, and it is entirely diagnostic: not one of those four weeks
told you what the system *should* do.

From next week we start turning the knob that actually decides the outcome —
and we begin by taking apart the rule you have believed in since you were four
years old.

</Beat>
