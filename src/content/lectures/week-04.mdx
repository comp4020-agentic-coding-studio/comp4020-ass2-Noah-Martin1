A passport office processes 800 applications a day. The average in-person wait
is 90 minutes.

That is 1,200 hours of human life per day, spent in a room, at that one office.
Over a working year it is roughly 300,000 hours — about 150 full-time
equivalents of people doing nothing. If you costed it at even a modest hourly
rate you would arrive at a number in the millions.

Now open the office's budget. That number is not in it. It is not in any line,
any forecast, any performance report. The office's costs are staff, premises and
systems; the 300,000 hours are borne entirely by people who are not party to any
of the office's decisions and have no way to bill anyone for them.

This is the central economic fact about queues, and everything in Act II depends
on it: **waiting is a real cost that is systematically invisible to the
organisation generating it.**

## Why that matters more than it sounds

An organisation optimises what it can see. When the cost of waiting sits
entirely outside its accounts, the rational internal decision is almost always
to push the queue rather than the budget — to run at 97% utilisation, to cut the
counter from four staff to three, to add a verification step that takes the
office thirty seconds and the queue forty minutes.

None of these are failures of management. Each is a correct decision given the
information the manager is being scored on. The failure is upstream, in what
gets measured, and this is why "the staff are lazy" is nearly always the wrong
diagnosis of a long queue.

## Whose hour is it?

Here is where the arithmetic gets uncomfortable.

If we cost waiting time at what people would pay to avoid it, we get a defensible
economic number — and we also get the result that a barrister's hour is worth
forty times a pensioner's. Any system that then allocates on that basis will
serve the barrister first, and it will have a rigorous efficiency argument for
doing so.

If instead we cost every hour identically, we have made a moral claim rather
than an economic measurement, and we should say so out loud rather than dress it
as arithmetic.

There is no neutral option here, including the option of not choosing, which in
practice means keeping whatever rule the system already had. This is the course's
thesis arriving on schedule: **there is no neutral queue.** By declining to
price waiting you have not avoided a distributional decision. You have made one
and declined to look at it.

Notice, too, who is actually standing in the passport queue at 10am on a
Tuesday. It is disproportionately people who cannot pay to avoid it, cannot send
somebody else, and cannot do it online. The queue is not a random sample of the
population, and any rule you apply to it lands on that population, not on an
average one.
