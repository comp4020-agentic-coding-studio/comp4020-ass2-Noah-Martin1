Sometime around 1970, banks in the United States did something that now looks
obvious and at the time needed explaining to customers on posters.

They replaced one queue per teller with a single queue feeding every teller.

Customers hated it. It looked longer. It *was* longer — one line of thirty
instead of six lines of five.

<Beat n={1} label="Guess the gain before you see it">

<Predict question="Six tellers, same customers, same speeds. What does merging the six lines into one actually buy?">

Both of the things you might have said, and the first one is bigger than nearly
everyone expects — including the version of me that wrote the first draft of
this lecture.

**It cuts the average wait, roughly in half.** Not by working faster. The
servers are identical. By never leaving a teller idle while somebody is
waiting.

**It destroys the variance.** In six lines you might pick the one behind the man
depositing a shoebox of coins. In one line that cannot happen: the order you
leave in is the order you arrived in.

I had assumed the historical gain was mostly about fairness and only marginally
about speed. That is a common belief and it is wrong, and the simulation below
is what corrected it.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Where the speed comes from">

It is worth being precise about this, because it is not obvious where a free
gain could possibly come from when nobody is working faster.

In six separate lines, a teller can be free while three people stand two metres
away in the next lane, because nobody switches fast enough to notice. That idle
time is pure waste. It is invisible to everybody in the building — the teller
looks like they are between customers, not like they are stranded — and it gets
worse as the system gets busier, which is exactly when you can least afford it.

A single line makes that state impossible. If anyone is waiting, no server is
idle. That is the whole mechanism.

One honest caveat: the simulation allows no lane-switching, which is the most
generous treatment of parallel queues on fairness and the least generous on
speed. Real shoppers jockey, and jockeying recovers part of the gap — never all
of it, because you can only switch after you have already lost the time that
told you to.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="The fairness claim, stated properly">

<Punch note="Transparent, auditable by anyone standing in it, and impossible to game except by arriving earlier.">
  Order of service equals order of arrival, and nothing else is considered.
</Punch>

That is a genuine commitment, and for a public system dealing with strangers
the properties are not small. It requires no information about the people in
it, which means it cannot discriminate on information it does not have.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="What it costs">

Three things, and Act II is going to spend a week on each of the first two.

**It ignores job length.** The person with a thirty-second query waits behind
the person with a twenty-minute mortgage restructure, paying twenty minutes for
a service worth half a minute to them. That is next week.

**It ignores urgency.** FCFS applied in an emergency department kills people.
Not hyperbole, and it is why no emergency department uses it. Week 7.

**It turns a queue into a test of free time.** This is the one nobody puts on
the poster. "First come" is only fair between people who can equally freely
come first. A queue that forms at 6am outside a housing office is allocating by
who can afford to lose a night's sleep and a morning's wages.

<Predict question="FCFS discriminates on nothing. So how does it manage to land unevenly?">

Because access to the rule is not distributed the way the rule assumes.

The rule's blindness is exactly what stops it noticing. It cannot see that the
person at the front took a day off and the person at the back could not,
because by design it cannot see anything except arrival order — and week 4
already told you who is actually standing in that room at 10am on a Tuesday.

A rule that considers nothing does not thereby become fair. It becomes fair
*conditional on everyone arriving on equal terms*, and that condition is
almost never checked.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="Why defaults matter most">

FCFS is the default. Defaults are the most powerful design decisions there are,
because they get chosen by nobody deciding.

For the next four weeks we replace it — with job length, with need, with price,
and finally with not queueing at all. Each replacement buys something real. Each
one charges somebody.

Your job, in the assignment due next week, is to make one of those trades
deliberately and then be honest about the bill.

</Beat>
