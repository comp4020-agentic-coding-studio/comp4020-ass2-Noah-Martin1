Sometime around 1970, banks in the United States did something that now looks
obvious and at the time was controversial enough to need explaining to
customers: they replaced one queue per teller with a single queue feeding all
tellers.

Customers hated it at first. It looked longer. It *was* longer — one line of
thirty instead of six lines of five. What it also was, and what nobody could see
by looking, was fairer and faster in the way that matters.

## What the single line actually does

Two things. The first one surprises people, including the version of me that
wrote the first draft of this lecture.

**It cuts the average wait, and not by a little.** The servers are identical and
working at identical speeds, so where does the gain come from? From never
leaving a server idle while somebody is waiting. In six separate lines a teller
can be free while three people stand two metres away in the next lane, because
nobody switches fast enough to notice. That idle time is pure waste; it is
invisible to everybody in the building; and it gets worse as the system gets
busier, which is exactly when you can least afford it.

The simulation below runs both against the same arrival stream, with the same
servers and the same service times. The pooled line comes in around twice as
fast on the mean. I had assumed, before running it, that the historical gain was
mostly about fairness and only marginally about speed. That is a common belief
and it is wrong.

One honest caveat: the simulation allows no lane-switching, which is the most
generous possible treatment of parallel queues in the fairness sense and the
least generous in the speed sense. Real shoppers jockey, and jockeying recovers
part of the gap — never all of it, because you can only switch after you have
already lost the time that told you to.

**It destroys the variance.** This is the part customers actually feel. In six
separate lines you might pick the one behind the man depositing a shoebox of
coins and wait four times as long as somebody who arrived after you. In one line
that cannot happen: the order you leave in is the order you arrived in. You have
swapped a lottery for a promise.

Watch the second graph as well as the first. The mean improves by roughly half;
the ninetieth percentile improves by considerably more. Most of the value of most
queue design is in the tail, and organisations almost invariably report the mean.

## The fairness claim, stated properly

FCFS says: **order of service equals order of arrival, and nothing else is
considered.**

That is a genuine and defensible commitment. It is transparent — anybody can
audit it by looking. It is manipulation-resistant; there is nothing to game
except arriving earlier. It requires no information about the people in it,
which means it cannot discriminate on information it does not have. For a
public system dealing with strangers, those are not small properties.

## What it costs

Three things, and you should be able to name all three by the end of the hour.

**It ignores job length.** The person with a thirty-second query waits behind
the person with a twenty-minute mortgage restructure. Every one of those
thirty-second customers pays twenty minutes for a service worth half a minute to
them. In aggregate this is enormous, and it is week 6's subject.

**It ignores urgency.** FCFS applied in an emergency department kills people.
This is not hyperbole and it is why no emergency department uses it. Week 7.

**It converts a queue into a test of free time.** "First come" is only fair
between people who can equally freely come first. A queue that forms at 6am
outside a housing office is allocating by who can afford to lose a night's sleep
and a morning's wages. The rule discriminates on nothing — but the *access* to
the rule is distributed very unevenly, and the rule's blindness is exactly what
prevents it from noticing.

## The point of Act II

FCFS is the default, and defaults are the most powerful design decisions there
are, because they get chosen by not deciding.

For the next four weeks we replace it — with job length, with need, with price,
and finally with not queueing at all. Each replacement buys something real. Each
one costs somebody. Your job, in the assignment due next week, is to make one of
those trades deliberately and then be honest about the bill.
