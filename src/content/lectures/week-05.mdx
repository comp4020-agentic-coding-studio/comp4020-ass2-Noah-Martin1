Sometime around 1970, banks in the United States did something that now looks
obvious and at the time needed explaining to customers on posters.

They replaced one queue per teller with a single queue feeding every teller.

Customers hated it. It looked longer. It *was* longer — one line of thirty
instead of six lines of five, and the thirty were the same thirty.

<Beat n={1} label="Guess the gain before you see it">

<Predict question="Four tellers, same customers, same speeds. What does merging the four lines into one actually buy?">

Both of the things you might have said, and the first one is far bigger than
nearly everyone expects — including the version of me who wrote the first draft
of this lecture and put "roughly a third" in it.

**It cuts the average visit from nineteen minutes to six.** Not by working
faster. The tellers are identical, and there are still four of them. By never
leaving one of them idle while somebody is waiting.

**It destroys the variance.** In four lines you might pick the one behind the
man depositing a shoebox of coins: the unluckiest tenth of customers wait
forty-three minutes. In one line that cannot happen, and the same unluckiest
tenth wait thirteen.

I had assumed the historical gain was mostly about fairness and only marginally
about speed. That is a common belief, it is wrong by a factor of three, and the
branch below is what corrected it.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Where the speed comes from">

It is worth being precise about this, because it is not obvious where a free
gain could possibly come from when nobody is working faster.

In four separate lines, a teller can be free while three people stand two
metres away in the next lane, because nobody switches fast enough to notice.
<Key>That idle time is pure waste, and it is invisible to everybody in the
building</Key> — the teller does not look stranded, they look like they are
between customers.

A single line makes that state impossible. If anyone is waiting, no window is
free. That is the whole mechanism, and the branch below counts it for you.

One honest caveat: nobody in the four lines switches lanes. That is the most
generous treatment of parallel queues on fairness and the least generous on
speed. Real customers jockey, and jockeying recovers part of the gap — never
all of it, because you can only switch after you have already lost the time
that told you to.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="The waste gets worse when the branch is quiet">

Turn the load down and watch the pink chip, because it does the opposite of
what it should.

At seventy customers an hour the branch strands thirty-seven teller-minutes
every hour. Take it down to a comfortable fifty-two and the waits all improve —
and the stranding rises to <Key>sixty-nine minutes an hour, more than one whole
teller permanently standing free beside a queue</Key>.

That is not a paradox once you say it out loud. Idle time is what a quiet
branch has more of, and every minute of it that coincides with somebody waiting
in the wrong lane is a minute nobody can ever get back. A manager looking at
this branch sees four busy-looking tellers and a reasonable wait. The rope is
eating a salary.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The fairness claim, stated properly">

<Punch note="Transparent, auditable by anyone standing in it, and impossible to game except by arriving earlier.">
  Order of service equals order of arrival, and nothing else is considered.
</Punch>

That is a genuine commitment, and for a public system dealing with strangers
the properties are not small. <Key>It requires no information about the people
in it, which means it cannot discriminate on information it does not have.</Key>

Notice what just happened, though. The single line is faster *and* it is the
one that keeps the promise: in four lines the order you leave in is decided by
which rope you guessed at. FCFS is not what four separate queues implement. It
is what one queue implements.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="What it costs">

Three things, and Act II is going to spend a week on each of the first two.

**It ignores job length.** The person with a thirty-second query waits behind
the person with a twenty-minute mortgage restructure, paying twenty minutes for
a service worth half a minute to them. That is next week.

**It ignores urgency.** FCFS applied in an emergency department kills people.
Not hyperbole, and it is why no emergency department uses it. Week 7.

**It turns a queue into a test of free time.** This is the one nobody puts on
the poster. "First come" is only fair between people who can equally freely
come first. A queue that forms at 6am outside a housing office is allocating by
who can afford to lose a night's sleep and a morning's wages.

<Predict question="FCFS discriminates on nothing. So how does it manage to land unevenly?">

Because access to the rule is not distributed the way the rule assumes.

The rule's blindness is exactly what stops it noticing. It cannot see that the
person at the front took a day off and the person at the back could not,
because by design it cannot see anything except arrival order.

<Key>A rule that considers nothing does not thereby become fair.</Key> It
becomes fair *conditional on everyone arriving on equal terms*, and that
condition is almost never checked.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Why defaults matter most">

FCFS is the default. Defaults are the most powerful design decisions there are,
because they get chosen by nobody deciding.

For the next four weeks we replace it — with job length, with need, with price,
and finally with not queueing at all. Each replacement buys something real. Each
one charges somebody.

Your job, in the assignment due next week, is to make one of those trades
deliberately and then be honest about the bill.

</Beat>
