Five people arrive at once. Their transactions take 1, 2, 3, 4 and 10 minutes.
One server.

Serve them in that order and the total waiting is 1+3+6+10 = 20 person-minutes,
average 4 minutes each.

Serve them longest-first and the total is 10+14+17+19 = 60, average 12.

Same five people. Same server. Same total work. Three times the waiting, purely
from the order.

## The result

**Shortest Job First minimises mean waiting time.** Not "usually", not "in
simulations" — provably, for any fixed set of jobs on a single server. The proof
is a two-line exchange argument: if a longer job is ever ahead of a shorter one,
swapping them reduces total wait, so the optimal order has no such pair, so it
is sorted by length.

This is the closest thing to a free lunch in the whole course, and it explains a
supermarket feature you have walked past ten thousand times. The express lane is
not a courtesy to people buying milk. It is a throughput device, and the
supermarket is the beneficiary.

## Why the free lunch is not free

Look again at the numbers. The averages moved. Where did the improvement come
from?

It came from the ten-minute customer. Under SJF they wait 10 minutes before
being served; under longest-first they wait none. **SJF pays for its excellent
average with somebody's tail**, and the person it charges is always the same
person: the one with the biggest job.

Now make the arrivals continuous rather than a single batch, and the problem
becomes structural. If short jobs keep arriving, the long job is never at the
front. It is not merely delayed; it can be **starved indefinitely**. A print
queue running strict SJF will hold your 400-page thesis behind an unending
trickle of one-page boarding passes.

This is why every real scheduler that uses SJF also uses something else: ageing
(a job's priority rises the longer it waits), or a reserved lane for long jobs,
or a hard cap. Those fixes cost some of the average you gained. That trade — how
much mean wait to give back in exchange for a bounded tail — is a genuine design
dial and there is no correct setting for it.

## The information problem

SJF has a prerequisite that FCFS does not: **you must know how long each job
will take, before you run it.**

Sometimes you do. A supermarket approximates it with basket size, which is
observable and roughly honest. A CPU cannot know, and so real operating systems
predict from recent history and are frequently wrong.

And where the estimate is self-reported, you have built an incentive. Tell
people short jobs go first and you will receive a great many jobs described as
short. The rule has created a strategic environment where none existed, and the
people best at gaming it are rarely the people the system most wanted to help.
That is week 11's subject, and it is the reason a rule that is optimal on paper
can be a disaster in a building.
