Five people arrive at a counter at the same moment. Their transactions take 1,
2, 3, 4 and 10 minutes. There is one server.

You cannot make anyone faster. You cannot add staff. You can only decide the
order.

<Beat n={1} label="Guess before you look">

<Predict question="How much difference can the order possibly make?">

**Three times** the total waiting.

Shortest first: 20 person-minutes of waiting. Longest first: 60. Same five
people, same server, same twenty minutes of work — the server is equally busy
in both, and finishes at exactly the same moment.

</Predict>

That is a batch of five, which is a puzzle. A shop is not a batch. People keep
arriving, and the till below is the same rule running all morning.

<Beat n={2} label="The result">

<Punch note="Provably, for any fixed set of jobs on a single server. Not usually, not in simulations.">
  Shortest job first minimises mean waiting time.
</Punch>

The proof is two lines. If a longer job is ever ahead of a shorter one, swapping
them reduces total waiting — the short job saves a lot, the long job loses only
a little. So the optimal order contains no such pair. So it is sorted by length.

This is the closest thing to a free lunch in the course, and it explains a
supermarket feature you have walked past ten thousand times. The express lane is
not a courtesy to people buying milk. It is a throughput device, and the
supermarket is the beneficiary.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="Find the person paying">

The averages moved. Somebody's did not.

Switch the till above to smallest-basket-first and watch who turns pink. It is
never a surprise. The counter over their head is the number of people who
arrived *after* them and have already been served and left, and on a busy
morning it does not come back down. Eighty items is a hundred ticks of work —
the lecture's ten-minute job, standing in a supermarket.

The readout prices it. At twenty shoppers an hour the mean visit falls from
seventeen minutes to eight and a half, and the full trolley's rises from
twenty-six to sixty.

<Key>The average did not improve. It was redistributed, and the people it was
taken from are visible from the door.</Key>

<Punch>
  SJF pays for its excellent average with somebody's tail, and it is always the
  same somebody: the one with the biggest job.
</Punch>

The scene above is already the continuous case, which is where the transfer
stops being a transfer and becomes structural. If short jobs keep arriving, the long job is
never at the front. It is not delayed — it is **starved**, indefinitely. A print
queue running strict SJF will hold your 400-page thesis behind an endless
trickle of one-page boarding passes, for ever, without anything going wrong.

Over a simulated month of trading at that till, the longest anybody waited was
an hour and three quarters under arrival order. Under smallest-first it was
<Key>thirteen hours and forty minutes</Key>. No queue collapsed, no staff went
home, no alarm fired anywhere in the system. That shopper simply never had the
smallest basket in the line.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The fix, and what the fix costs">

Every real scheduler that uses SJF also uses something else: **ageing**, where a
job's priority rises the longer it has waited. Or a reserved lane for long jobs.
Or a hard cap.

Each of those gives back some of the average you gained. How much mean wait to
return in exchange for a bounded worst case is a genuine design dial, there is
no correct setting for it, and you will plot that exact trade-off curve for
yourself in this week's lab.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="The prerequisite nobody mentions">

SJF needs something FCFS never asks for: **you must know how long each job will
take, before you run it.**

Sometimes you can. A supermarket approximates it with basket size, which is
observable and roughly honest. A CPU cannot, so real operating systems predict
from recent history and are frequently wrong.

<Predict question="What happens when the estimate is self-reported?">

You have just built an incentive.

Tell people short jobs go first and you will receive a great many jobs described
as short. The rule has created a strategic environment where none existed, and
the people best at exploiting it are rarely the people the system most wanted to
help.

This is week 11's subject, and it is the reason a rule that is optimal on paper
can be a disaster in a building. Hold the thought: a rule that is optimal when
obeyed is often worse than a rule that is merely decent when gamed.

</Predict>

</Beat>
