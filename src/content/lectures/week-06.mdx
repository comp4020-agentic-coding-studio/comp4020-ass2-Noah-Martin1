Five people arrive at a counter at the same moment. Their transactions take 1,
2, 3, 4 and 10 minutes. There is one server.

You cannot make anyone faster. You cannot add staff. You can only decide the
order.

<Beat n={1} label="Guess before you look">

<Predict question="How much difference can the order possibly make?">

**Three times** the total waiting.

Shortest first: 20 person-minutes of waiting. Longest first: 60. Same five
people, same server, same twenty minutes of work — the server is equally busy
in both, and finishes at exactly the same moment.

</Predict>

That is a batch of five, which is a puzzle. A shop is not a batch. People keep
arriving, and the till below is the same rule running all morning.

<Beat n={2} label="The result">

<Punch note="Provably, for any fixed set of jobs on a single server. Not usually, not in simulations.">
  Shortest job first minimises mean waiting time.
</Punch>

The proof is two lines, and it is worth being able to reproduce it, because it
is the only unarguable thing in Act II.

Take any order at all. If a longer job sits anywhere ahead of a shorter one,
swap that adjacent pair. The short job now finishes earlier by the length of the
long one; the long job finishes later by the length of the short one. Since the
long one is longer, the saving beats the loss, and the total waiting has gone
*down*. So an optimal order contains no adjacent out-of-order pair — which means
it is sorted, shortest first.

That is an **exchange argument**, and it is the standard tool for this whole
family of results. Notice what it does not need: no probability, no assumption
about arrivals, no simulation. It is a statement about arithmetic.

This is the closest thing to a free lunch in the course, and it explains a
supermarket feature you have walked past ten thousand times. The express lane is
not a courtesy to people buying milk. It is a throughput device, and the
supermarket is the beneficiary.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="Find the person paying">

The averages moved. Somebody's did not.

Switch the till above to smallest-basket-first and watch who turns pink. It is
never a surprise. The counter over their head is the number of people who
arrived *after* them and have already been served and left, and on a busy
morning it does not come back down. Eighty items is a hundred ticks of work —
the lecture's ten-minute job, standing in a supermarket.

The readout prices it. At twenty shoppers an hour the mean visit falls from
seventeen minutes to eight and a half, and the full trolley's rises from
twenty-six to sixty.

<Key>The average did not improve. It was redistributed, and the people it was
taken from are visible from the door.</Key>

<Punch>
  SJF pays for its excellent average with somebody's tail, and it is always the
  same somebody: the one with the biggest job.
</Punch>

The scene above is already the continuous case, which is where the transfer
stops being a transfer and becomes structural. If short jobs keep arriving, the long job is
never at the front. It is not delayed — it is **starved**, indefinitely. A print
queue running strict SJF will hold your 400-page thesis behind an endless
trickle of one-page boarding passes, for ever, without anything going wrong.

Over a simulated month of trading at that till, the longest anybody waited was
an hour and three quarters under arrival order. Under smallest-first it was
<Key>thirteen hours and forty minutes</Key>. No queue collapsed, no staff went
home, no alarm fired anywhere in the system. That shopper simply never had the
smallest basket in the line.

Notice the shape of that failure, because it is the shape of most of them.
Nothing broke. Every individual decision the till made was locally correct and
defensible, the average improved, the throughput was unchanged, and the harm
landed entirely in a percentile nobody was looking at.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The whole family, and where it was worked out">

Ordering by length is one member of a family, and the place the family was
worked out properly is not a supermarket. It is the inside of an operating
system, where the queue is processes, the server is a CPU core, and the
scheduler makes this decision thousands of times a second with nobody to
complain to.

Five rules, in the order they were invented, each fixing the previous one's
worst failure:

**First come, first served.** Last week. Simple, fair, and vulnerable to one
long job at the front stalling everything behind it. Operating-system people
call that **convoy effect**, which is a good name for something you have also
experienced behind a trolley.

**Shortest job first.** This week. Best possible mean. Starves the long jobs.

**Shortest remaining processing time.** SJF, but allowed to interrupt: if a
shorter job arrives while a longer one is running, switch. It is provably the
best possible policy for mean response time, and it makes the starvation
*worse*, because now the long job can be pushed back off the server it had
already reached. There is no free improvement here — the better the average, the
harder the tail.

**Round robin.** Give everybody a slice in turn. Nobody is optimal, nobody
starves, and the mean is worse than SJF by a wide margin. In the limit of tiny
slices it becomes **processor sharing**, where every job progresses at a rate
proportional to how many are present — which is, incidentally, roughly what a
motorway does, and roughly what a broadband link does, and exactly why both feel
fair and slow.

**Multi-level feedback queues.** The clever one, and the reason your laptop
feels responsive. New jobs start in a high-priority queue with a short slice. A
job that uses its whole slice gets demoted to a lower-priority queue with a
longer one. A job that gives the CPU back early stays high.

<Predict question="What is that last rule actually doing?">

It is running shortest-job-first **without being told the job lengths**.

It does not ask. It *observes*: a job that keeps finishing its slice early is
behaving like a short job, so it keeps its priority. A job that burns every
slice it is given is behaving like a long job, and gets demoted.

Which is a completely general design move, and the most useful thing in this
lecture. <Key>When you need information the queue does not have, you can
sometimes infer it from behaviour instead of demanding it.</Key> Behaviour
cannot be misreported as easily as a form can.

And the same scheduler solves the starvation, with the second general move:
periodically it promotes everything back to the top. That is **ageing**, and it
is the next beat.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="The fix, and what the fix costs">

Every real system that uses SJF also uses something else, because unbounded
starvation is not shippable.

**Ageing.** A job's priority rises the longer it has waited, so the full trolley
eventually beats the basket. This is what an OS scheduler does, what a hospital
does informally to its lowest triage category, and what any support-ticket
system with an "escalates after 5 days" rule is doing.

**A reserved lane.** Un-pool a server and dedicate it to long jobs. This is
week 5's pooling gain, spent deliberately. The express lane is this trick
pointed the other way.

**A hard cap.** Nobody waits more than N. Simple, auditable, and it converts an
unbounded tail into a bounded one at a known price.

Each gives back some of the average you gained, and the trade is a genuine dial
rather than a bug to be fixed. Turn ageing up and you approach FCFS: fair,
bounded, slower on average. Turn it down and you approach pure SJF: fast on
average, with somebody in the corner who will be there at closing time.

<Key>There is no correct setting, only a defensible one</Key> — and "defensible"
means you can say which percentile you chose to protect and what the mean cost
you paid for it. You will plot that exact curve for yourself in this week's lab,
and the shape of it is the answer to the assignment's hardest question.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="The prerequisite nobody mentions">

SJF needs something FCFS never asks for: **you must know how long each job will
take, before you run it.**

That requirement is doing far more work than it looks, and there are only three
ways to satisfy it.

**Observe a proxy.** Basket size. Page count. File size. Number of line items on
the form. Cheap, roughly honest, and the reason the express lane is defined by
*items* rather than by *minutes* — items are countable from two metres away by
a person who is not paying attention, which is a real engineering constraint.

**Predict from history.** This job looks like jobs that took four minutes.
Operating systems do this; so does every call centre that routes on the caller's
account type. It works while the future resembles the past, and it fails
silently the moment somebody new arrives.

**Ask.** And here the trouble starts.

<Predict question="What happens when the estimate is self-reported?">

You have just built an incentive.

Tell people short jobs go first and you will receive a great many jobs described
as short. The rule has created a strategic environment where none existed, and
the people best at exploiting it are rarely the people the system most wanted to
help.

The supermarket's version is beautifully mundane: the *ten items or fewer* sign
is gamed by splitting a shop into two baskets and a partner, and by the
long-running argument about whether six yoghurts are one item. That is a
priority system being negotiated in a car park.

This is week 11's subject, and it is the reason a rule that is optimal on paper
can be a disaster in a building. Hold the thought: a rule that is optimal when
obeyed is often worse than a rule that is merely decent when gamed.

</Predict>

One more thing about estimates, which is the bridge to next week. A proxy is not
the truth — basket size is *correlated* with service time, not equal to it, and
a trolley of fifteen awkward items can beat a basket of nine. So every real SJF
system is running on a **classifier**, and its performance is bounded by how
good that classifier is.

<Punch note="Next week runs exactly this experiment in a place where a mis-sorted job is a person having a heart attack.">
  Every rule that beats first-come-first-served does so by using information
  first-come-first-served ignores — which means every one of them can be wrong
  about that information.
</Punch>

</Beat>
