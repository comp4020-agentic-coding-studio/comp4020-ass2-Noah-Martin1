other. A sprained wrist, a sore throat, a cut hand, back pain, chest pain, a
rash. Two treatment bays, and a nurse with about ninety seconds to decide the
order.

Nobody argues for arrival order here. The chest pain goes first, and if you
asked the wrist — who arrived an hour earlier and will now wait a further half
hour — they would agree.

That unanimity is remarkable and worth interrogating, because it is the only
place in this course where you will find it.

</Beat>

<Predict question="Every other priority rule in this course gets resisted. Why does this one get consent?">

Because the outcomes being traded are not the same kind of thing.

Week 6's shortest-job-first also reordered people, also improved the average,
also charged somebody — and people resent it, split their baskets to game it,
and complain about the express lane. Here the wrist gives up thirty minutes of
discomfort and the chest pain keeps their life. Those are not commensurable, so
nobody is tempted to compare them.

Which gives you a general and slightly cynical rule for predicting how much
resistance a priority scheme will meet: **it depends less on the logic of the
rule than on how unlike each other the consequences are.** When everybody in
the queue is merely waiting, priority feels like queue-jumping and gets fought.

</Predict>

<Beat n={2} label="What triage actually is">

A priority queue with a fixed number of classes. Within a class, arrival order.
Between classes, strict priority.

The Australasian Triage Scale uses five, and the thing that makes it a real
instrument rather than a vibe is that each category comes with a **target time
to treatment**:

| Category | Meaning | Target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Immediately life-threatening | Immediate |
| 2 | Imminently life-threatening | 10 minutes |
| 3 | Potentially life-threatening | 30 minutes |
| 4 | Potentially serious | 60 minutes |
| 5 | Less urgent | 120 minutes |

Other countries run near-identical instruments under different names — the
Manchester Triage System, the Emergency Severity Index — and the convergence is
itself informative. Several health systems, working separately, arrived at
roughly five classes with roughly these targets.

The target is what turns a wait into a **failure**. A category 2 patient at
twelve minutes is a department having a bad afternoon. A category 1 patient at
twelve minutes is the thing the entire apparatus exists to prevent. Notice that
this is a completely different way of reporting a queue from anything in Act I:
not a mean, not a percentile, but <Key>a rate of breaching a promise that was
made per person</Key>.

The scene above collapses five categories into three, so that the room stays
readable. Everything that follows is true of five.

Two properties do the real work.

**It is preemptive at the front.** A category 1 arriving at minute 59 of your
wait goes ahead of you, and there is no cap on how often that can happen. Your
wait is not a queue position; it is a queue position that can be invalidated at
any moment by an ambulance.

**It sorts on information FCFS throws away.** Arrival order is a fact about the
queue. Urgency is a fact about the person. That is the whole of the gain, and it
is week 6's lesson again: the rules that beat first-come-first-served all do it
by using information first-come-first-served ignores.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="Where the idea came from">

Worth two minutes, because the origin explains the shape.

The word is French — *trier*, to sort — and the practice is usually traced to
**Dominique Jean Larrey**, Napoleon's surgeon-in-chief, who organised battlefield
care around a principle that was genuinely radical at the time: treat by
severity of wound, and disregard rank.

Sit with that for a second. The rule triage replaced was not first-come-first-
served. It was *sort by social position*. The innovation was not "use need" so
much as "**stop using something else**", and it had to be imposed by an
institution against the interests of the people it demoted.

The modern mass-casualty version, START and its relatives, keeps the structure
and adds the part nobody likes to discuss. Four tags: immediate, delayed, minor
— and a fourth for the patients who will not survive with the resources
available, who are therefore not treated first even though they are the most
ill.

<Punch note="Which is the clearest statement in the whole course that a priority rule encodes an objective, and that different objectives produce opposite orderings from identical facts.">
  Sickest-first and most-lives-saved are different rules, and in a disaster
  they disagree.
</Punch>

In a normally resourced department they mostly agree, which is why the
distinction stays invisible and why everybody can support triage without ever
being asked which of the two they support. Week 12 is the lecture where that
question finally has to be answered out loud.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The same rule, outside a hospital">

Once you can see the pattern — fixed classes, a target per class, strict
priority between, arrival order within — you find it running in places with no
clinical connection at all.

**Ambulance dispatch.** A call handler, working from a script, assigns a
category before anybody has seen the patient, and the category determines the
response-time standard the service is measured against. It is triage performed
over a phone line by somebody who cannot see the patient, on information from a
caller who is frightened. Everything in Priya's half of this lecture applies to
it twice over.

**Incident severity.** Every operations team on earth runs P1 to P4 with a
response target attached to each, which is the Australasian Triage Scale with
servers instead of patients. The failure modes are identical, including the
famous one: everything gets filed as a P1 by whoever raised it.

**Manufacturing and repair.** A production line down is a category 1. A
degraded machine is a category 3. The targets are in the maintenance contract.

**Content moderation and safety queues.** Reports are classified on arrival and
routed by severity, by a classifier that is often literally a model, at a scale
where nobody can audit an individual decision.

<Key>The medical version is the one with the best-developed ethics and the
worst-tolerated failures</Key>, which is why it is worth studying even if you
will never work in a hospital. Everybody else is running the same machine with
less scrutiny.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="Priya's half of the lecture">

Everything above treats the category as given.

It is not given. It is **produced** — in about ninety seconds, by a tired human
with incomplete information — and the system's performance is bounded by the
quality of that judgement, not by the elegance of the queue discipline sitting
on top of it.

Three failure modes, all documented.

**Under-triage of atypical presentation.** Category descriptions encode a
prototype. Patients who do not present like the prototype get sorted down.
Women having heart attacks are the canonical study.

**Under-reading of pain in people accustomed to not being believed.** The
measurement instrument is a conversation, and conversations carry every bias
the participants brought with them.

**The bottom category's wait is unbounded.** Strict priority in a busy
department means the least urgent patients can be functionally starved — the
same mathematics as last week's trolley, with much worse consequences.
Departments handle it with ageing rules, exactly as schedulers do, and the
rules are largely informal.

There is a fourth, and it belongs to the institution rather than the nurse.
**The classifier is an input to the performance report.** When a department is
measured on the share of category 2 patients seen within ten minutes, the
category becomes something the department has an interest in. Nobody has to be
dishonest for this to bend: a borderline case carded down is a borderline case
that cannot breach, and there is always a defensible clinical story for either
choice.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="What a bad classifier actually costs">

<Predict question="Your classifier is 15% wrong. How much does the queue degrade?">

Nothing like 15% — and in the wrong places entirely.

Take the department above from a perfect classifier to a 92% one. The mean wait
for a category 1 patient goes from six minutes to nine. Category 3 gets
**shorter**, from 98 minutes to 91, because the mis-sorted are now sitting in
front of them. A monthly report containing those three numbers would not raise
an eyebrow anywhere.

Here is what actually happened. The share of category 1 patients waiting more
than half an hour goes from 0.7% to 3.0%: <Key>four times as many people having
heart attacks in the waiting room</Key>. Drop the classifier to 84% and it
nearly doubles again.

<Punch note="It concentrates on the small group sorted downwards, who then wait under the wrong category's target with the wrong condition.">
  The cost of a bad classifier does not spread.
</Punch>

An average is exactly the wrong instrument for finding this, which is why the
readout above carries a rate over the tail instead. You will produce your own
error rate in this week's lab, and then apply it.

</Predict>

Two consequences follow, and they generalise past hospitals.

**Errors in one direction are not errors in the other.** Carding a sprained
wrist as a category 2 costs somebody thirty minutes. Carding a heart attack as a
category 3 can cost a life. A classifier reported as "92% accurate" has told you
almost nothing, because that single number has averaged over two failures that
are not remotely comparable. Ask for the confusion matrix, always, and ask which
cell is the one that kills.

**A sorting rule is only ever as good as the thing it sorts on** — which means
that every time you improve the *rule*, you should ask whether the same effort
spent on the *measurement* would buy more. A department choosing between a
cleverer queue discipline and a second triage nurse is usually looking at a
false choice in which the second option wins.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} label="The disagreement, stated plainly">

Adaeze's framing is that the ordering rule is where the distributive work
happens.

Priya's is that the rule is only ever as good as the measurement feeding it, and
that <Key>a beautifully designed priority queue fed by a biased classifier is a
machine for laundering that bias into something that looks like clinical
objectivity</Key>.

The scene refuses to settle it, and the two readings of its readout are why.

Read down the arrival-order row: 53% of category 1 patients wait more than half
an hour. Under a 92%-accurate triage, 3.0% do. <Key>Triage is not a marginal
improvement — it is the difference between half of them and one in thirty.</Key>
Adaeze's framing wins that comparison and it is not close.

Read across the accuracy rows and you get Priya's. Every one of that 3.0% is a
patient the department looked at, assessed, gave a number to, and sent to a
chair. Arrival order never claimed to have done any of that. A queue that
promises nothing cannot mis-sort you; the promise is what creates the failure
mode, and the promise is still worth making.

We do not resolve this, and you should not expect the course to resolve it for
you. It is the live question in the field, and the final design brief is marked
partly on whether you noticed that your own design has a classifier in it
somewhere.

Next week: a rule that sorts not on need, but on what you are willing to pay.

</Beat>
