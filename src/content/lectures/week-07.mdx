Nobody argues for first-come-first-served in an emergency department. The chest
pain goes before the sprained wrist, and if you asked the person with the wrist
they would agree, even though they arrived first and will now wait four hours.

This is the clearest case in the course of a society explicitly rejecting
arrival order — and it is worth asking why the consent is so total here and so
absent elsewhere.

## What triage is

A priority queue with a fixed number of classes. The Australasian Triage Scale
uses five, from *immediately life-threatening* to *less urgent*, each with a
target time to treatment. Within a class, arrival order. Between classes,
strict priority.

Two properties are doing the work.

**It is preemptive at the front.** A category 1 arriving at minute 59 of your
wait goes ahead of you, and there is no cap on how often that can happen.

**It reorders on a dimension FCFS cannot see.** Arrival order is information
about the queue. Urgency is information about the person. Any rule that improves
on FCFS does so by using information FCFS throws away, and the whole question is
whether you can obtain that information cheaply, accurately, and without
creating an incentive to fake it.

## Where the free lunch is

Unlike SJF, triage does not really trade average for tail — it trades *whose*
tail. The wrist waits longer so that the heart attack does not die. Almost
everyone accepts that trade, because the outcomes being traded are radically
unequal in kind: four hours of discomfort against a life.

That asymmetry is the reason consent is so total here, and it does not transfer.
When the outcomes are commensurable — everyone is just waiting — priority feels
like queue-jumping, and it is resisted fiercely. The strength of your ordering
rule's mandate depends less on its logic than on how unlike each other the
consequences are.

## Where it breaks — Priya's half of the lecture

Everything above treats the category as given. It is not given. It is produced,
in about ninety seconds, by a tired human with incomplete information, and the
system's performance is bounded by the quality of that judgement rather than by
the elegance of the queue discipline sitting on top of it.

Three failure modes, all documented:

**Under-triage of atypical presentation.** Category descriptions encode a
prototype. Patients who do not present like the prototype are systematically
sorted down. Women having heart attacks are the canonical study.

**Under-reading of pain in people accustomed to not being believed.** The
measurement instrument is a conversation, and conversations carry every bias the
participants brought with them.

**The category-4 wait is unbounded.** Strict priority with a busy department
means the least-urgent patients can be functionally starved — the same
mathematics as the thesis stuck behind the boarding passes, with worse
consequences. Departments handle this with ageing rules, exactly as schedulers
do, and the rules are largely informal.

## The disagreement, stated plainly

Adaeze's framing is that the ordering rule is where the distributive work
happens. Priya's is that the rule is only as good as the measurement it runs on,
and that a beautifully designed priority queue fed by a biased classifier is a
machine for laundering that bias into something that looks like clinical
objectivity.

We do not resolve this in the lecture, and you should not expect the course to
resolve it for you. It is the live question in the field, and the final design
brief will be marked partly on whether you noticed that your own design has a
classifier in it somewhere.
