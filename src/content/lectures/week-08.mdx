Disney ran FastPass free for two decades. In 2021 it became Genie+ at $15 a
day. In October 2022 it gained surge pricing. In July 2024 it became Lightning
Lane Multi Pass at $20–$45 a person a day, and that autumn Disney added Premier
Pass — every Lightning Lane in the park, once each — at
<Key>$329 to $449 a person a day at Magic Kingdom</Key>, several times the price
of getting in.

Hold on to the surge pricing, because it is the tell. The pass costs most on
the days the standby line is worst. <Key>The thing being sold is the length of
somebody else's queue.</Key>

People find this objectionable in a way they do not find the admission charge
objectionable. It is worth working out exactly what the objection is, because
the obvious version of it does not survive contact with week 4.

<Beat n={1} label="The uncomfortable starting point">

You already paid to be at the front. You paid in minutes.

<Punch note="A queue is an auction denominated in time. The people who reach the front are the people willing to bid ninety minutes of their life.">
  Every queue is already sorting by willingness to pay.
</Punch>

<Predict question="So what exactly is good about time as the currency?">

Not that it is equally distributed — week 4 established that it is not, and that
who is standing in the room at 10am on a Tuesday is not a random sample.

And here is the part that should genuinely bother you: **the time spent in a
queue is burned.** When you pay ninety minutes, nobody receives ninety minutes.
The value is destroyed. When you pay forty dollars, the park receives forty
dollars, and can in principle hire more staff, lower admission, or open another
ride.

That is the economist's case and it is strong. Congestion charging in Singapore,
Stockholm and London rests on exactly this argument, and it works: the traffic
falls and the revenue funds transit.

</Predict>

Notice what the two currencies have in common and where they part. Both are
auctions. Both allocate a scarce thing to whoever bids most. The difference is
entirely in **what happens to the bid**: a queue burns it, a price transfers it.
Economists call the burned part **deadweight loss**, and a queue is one of the
purest generators of it in ordinary life — an allocation mechanism whose entire
operating cost is destroyed human time.

That is the strongest argument for pricing, and you should be able to state it
in one sentence before you attack it: <Key>a queue and a price sort the same
people in the same order, and only one of them sets the money on fire.</Key>

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Three objections, hardest last">

**It sorts by wealth, not valuation.** Willingness to pay is willingness *and
ability*. Forty dollars from someone with forty thousand is not the same signal
as forty dollars from someone with four hundred, so the auction is not measuring
how much people want the ride. It measures what they have. Time, for all its
faults, is distributed far more equally than money — everybody gets
twenty-four hours, and nobody can buy a twenty-fifth.

That last clause is the strongest version of the argument and it is worth
stress-testing, because it is not quite true. People *can* buy time: childcare,
a taxi instead of a bus, somebody else to stand in the line. The correct
statement is weaker and still decisive — **time is distributed far more equally
than money, and converting time into money is far easier in one direction than
the other.**

**It creates a reason not to fix the queue.** This is the one that should worry
you most. A park's revenue from priority passes depends on the standard queue
being unpleasant. Once a system earns money from congestion it has acquired an
interest in preserving it — and this applies to airport security, motorway
lanes and customer support alike. Surge pricing on a fast pass is that interest
written down as a pricing rule.

**It reclassifies the good.** Some queues are for things we have decided should
not be allocated by price at all: organ transplants, emergency care, a place at
a public school. Introducing payment does not just reallocate them, it changes
what kind of thing they are. A blood donation system that pays donors is not the
same institution as one that does not, even if the litres match. That is
week 12.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="The measurement everybody skips">

Here is the empirical question almost no public argument about fast lanes
bothers to ask: **what actually happens to the standard queue?**

There are two cases and they give opposite answers.

If the paid lane uses **spare** capacity — a counter that was sitting idle —
everyone is better off and the objection is purely symbolic.

If the paid lane uses **the same servers**, and it usually does, every skip is
somebody else's delay.

<Predict question="Ten per cent buy priority, sharing the same servers. What happens to the population's average wait?">

Nothing. Not "almost nothing" — nothing.

Drag the slider below from 0 to 80 per cent and watch the fourth number in the
readout. It reads 23.3 minutes at every single setting. That is not the
simulation being insensitive; it is a conservation law. <Key>Priority is
work-conserving: nobody serves faster and nobody goes home, so the mean wait
across everybody cannot change. Reordering can only move it between
people.</Key>

Which means an operator who reports that the fast lane did not raise the
average wait is stating a theorem, not presenting evidence. It would have been
true before they built it.

Look at the standby group's ninetieth percentile instead. It goes from 54
minutes to 75 when three in ten buy a pass, and to nearly four hours at eighty
per cent. That is where the transfer went, and an average is structurally
incapable of showing it to you — the same trap as last week's classifier, in a
park instead of a hospital.

</Predict>

The conservation law is worth one more sentence, because it is the single most
transferable fact in Act II. **Any work-conserving reordering of a queue leaves
the mean wait exactly where it was.** Week 6's shortest-job-first appears to
break this and does not — it lowers the mean *per customer* only because it
front-loads the short jobs, and the total person-minutes of waiting is what
falls. Priority by payment does not even do that: rich and poor jobs are the
same length. It is a pure transfer, and the fact that it is *pure* is precisely
why no efficiency argument can defend it once the lane shares the servers.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="Where this has actually been done, and what happened">

Theme parks are the easy case because nobody has to ride. Roads are the hard
case, and the record is better than most people assume.

**Singapore, 1975.** The Area Licensing Scheme: a paper licence to enter the
central district in the morning peak, checked by people standing at the
roadside. Electronic Road Pricing replaced it in 1998, with gantries and
variable rates by time of day and level of congestion. Fifty years of continuous
operation, which makes it the longest-running natural experiment available.

**London, February 2003.** A flat daily charge to drive into the centre.
Politically survivable because the traffic effect was immediate and visible, and
because the revenue was hypothecated to buses.

**Stockholm, 2006.** The most instructive of the three, because of the order
events happened in. A **trial** ran from January to July. A **referendum**
followed in September. The charge was made permanent the year after — and the
reason it passed is that support for it was substantially higher *after* people
had lived with it than before. Asked in the abstract, a charge to drive is a
tax. Asked after a summer of measurably shorter journeys, it is a different
question.

**New York, January 2025.** The first such scheme in the United States, after
roughly two decades of proposals.

Two lessons come out of that list, and neither is "pricing works".

<Punch note="Singapore, London and Stockholm all recycled the money visibly into the alternative to driving. The schemes that fail are the ones where the revenue disappears into a general fund.">
  What makes a price acceptable is not the price. It is where the money goes.
</Punch>

And the second: **experience moves opinion in one direction.** The abstract
version of a fast lane is universally disliked; the lived version is frequently
tolerated. That is an uncomfortable fact for anyone designing one, because it
means the honest way to find out is to run it, and running it is the part you
cannot take back.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="The decay nobody advertises">

Push the slider past 50%.

The advantage of the fast lane collapses, because the fast lane is now a queue.
At 80% uptake a pass buys you a 5.6 minute wait instead of a 3.0 minute one,
and it costs the same.
At high uptake you have a pure transfer from customers to operator with almost
no change in service — everybody has paid, and nobody is faster.

This has a name in the wild — **priority inflation** — and once you look for it
you will find it everywhere that a fast lane has been a commercial success.
Airport expedited screening works beautifully until enough people enrol.
Priority boarding is a queue of its own on most flights now. A support tier
labelled "premium" stops meaning anything the moment it is the majority tier.

<Key>The value of a fast lane is not a property of the lane. It is a property of
how few people are in it</Key> — which means an operator selling access to it
has an incentive that is in direct conflict with the product they are selling,
and the conflict only resolves in one direction.

Find that point for a system you use. It is the number the operator most wants
to know and least wants published, and finding it is a legitimate basis for
your simulation report.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="A checklist for the next one you meet">

You will be in the room for one of these arguments within five years of leaving
here. Four questions, in order, and they are the entire lecture.

**1. Is the fast lane using spare capacity or shared capacity?** If spare, most
objections evaporate. If shared, every skip is somebody's delay and you should
say so out loud.

**2. Who is in the slow lane?** Week 4's question. If the standard queue is
disproportionately people who could not have paid, the scheme is a transfer from
them, and the fact that they "chose" the free option is not a defence.

**3. Where does the revenue go?** Into capacity that shortens the queue for
everybody, or into margin that depends on the queue staying long? This is the
single best predictor of whether a scheme survives contact with the public.

**4. What is the uptake at which it stops working, and who is watching it?**

<Predict question="A hospital proposes paid priority for elective surgery, using the same operating theatres. Run the checklist.">

It fails at question one and never recovers.

Shared capacity, so every private slot is an NHS slot that did not happen, and
the mean wait across everybody is fixed by the conservation law — the total is
untouched and the distribution moves toward whoever paid.

Question two makes it worse: the people in the slow lane are, by construction,
the ones who could not pay. Question three is where it becomes interesting
rather than simply objectionable — a scheme whose revenue demonstrably funds
additional theatre capacity is a different proposal, and worth arguing about on
its merits.

And question four is the one that gets skipped: at what uptake does the paid
list become its own waiting list? Nobody proposing these publishes that number,
because nobody has computed it.

<Key>Notice that you did not need an opinion about healthcare to get this far.
You needed four questions and a conservation law.</Key>

</Predict>

</Beat>
