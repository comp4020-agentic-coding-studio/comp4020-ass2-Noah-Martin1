A theme park sells a pass that lets you skip the line.

People find this objectionable in a way they do not find the admission charge
objectionable. It is worth working out exactly what the objection is, because
the obvious version of it does not survive contact with week 4.

<Beat n={1} label="The uncomfortable starting point">

You already paid to be at the front. You paid in minutes.

<Punch note="A queue is an auction denominated in time. The people who reach the front are the people willing to bid ninety minutes of their life.">
  Every queue is already sorting by willingness to pay.
</Punch>

<Predict question="So what exactly is good about time as the currency?">

Not that it is equally distributed — week 4 established that it is not, and that
who is standing in the room at 10am on a Tuesday is not a random sample.

And here is the part that should genuinely bother you: **the time spent in a
queue is burned.** When you pay ninety minutes, nobody receives ninety minutes.
The value is destroyed. When you pay forty dollars, the park receives forty
dollars, and can in principle hire more staff, lower admission, or open another
ride.

That is the economist's case and it is strong. Congestion charging in Singapore,
Stockholm and London rests on exactly this argument, and it works: the traffic
falls and the revenue funds transit.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Three objections, hardest last">

**It sorts by wealth, not valuation.** Willingness to pay is willingness *and
ability*. Forty dollars from someone with forty thousand is not the same signal
as forty dollars from someone with four hundred, so the auction is not measuring
how much people want the ride. It measures what they have. Time, for all its
faults, is distributed far more equally than money.

**It creates a reason not to fix the queue.** This is the one that should worry
you most. A park's revenue from priority passes depends on the standard queue
being unpleasant. Once a system earns money from congestion it has acquired an
interest in preserving it — and this applies to airport security, motorway
lanes and customer support alike.

**It reclassifies the good.** Some queues are for things we have decided should
not be allocated by price at all: organ transplants, emergency care, a place at
a public school. Introducing payment does not just reallocate them, it changes
what kind of thing they are. That is week 12.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} label="The measurement everybody skips">

Here is the empirical question almost no public argument about fast lanes
bothers to ask: **what actually happens to the standard queue?**

There are two cases and they give opposite answers.

If the paid lane uses **spare** capacity — a counter that was sitting idle —
everyone is better off and the objection is purely symbolic.

If the paid lane uses **the same servers**, and it usually does, every skip is
somebody else's delay.

<Predict question="Ten per cent buy priority, sharing the same servers. What happens to the population's average wait?">

Almost nothing. It barely moves.

Which is exactly why an operator can say, with a straight face and without
lying, that the fast lane did not make things worse.

Look at the unpaid group's ninetieth percentile instead. That is where the cost
went, and an average is structurally incapable of showing it to you — the same
trap as week 7's classifier, in a different building.

</Predict>

<Beat n={4} label="The decay nobody advertises">

Push the slider past 50%.

The advantage of the fast lane collapses, because the fast lane is now a queue.
At high uptake you have a pure transfer from customers to operator with almost
no change in service — everybody has paid, and nobody is faster.

Find that point for a system you use. It is the number the operator most wants
to know and least wants published, and finding it is a legitimate basis for
your simulation report.

having the queue at all.
