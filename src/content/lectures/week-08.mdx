A theme park sells a pass that lets you skip the line. People find this
objectionable in a way they do not find objectionable that the park charges
admission at all, and it is worth working out exactly what the objection is,
because the obvious version of it does not survive contact with week 4.

## The argument for

Start from where week 4 left us. A queue is already a price. Standing in it for
ninety minutes costs you ninety minutes, and the people who get to the front are
the people willing to pay that. **A queue is an auction denominated in time.**

Which raises the awkward question: what, exactly, is good about time as a
currency?

It is not that time is equally distributed — it is not. It is not that time is
harmless — week 4 costed it at millions. And critically, **the time spent in a
queue is burned**. When you pay ninety minutes, nobody receives ninety minutes.
The value is destroyed. When you pay forty dollars, the park receives forty
dollars, and can in principle hire more staff, lower admission, or open another
ride.

This is the economist's case and it is genuinely strong: replacing a
time-auction with a money-auction converts pure waste into transferable value.
Congestion charging in Singapore, Stockholm and London rests on exactly this
argument, and it works — the traffic falls, and the revenue funds transit.

## The argument against

Three objections, in ascending order of how hard they are to answer.

**It sorts by wealth rather than valuation.** Willingness to pay is willingness
*and ability*. Forty dollars from someone with forty thousand is not the same
signal as forty dollars from someone with four hundred, so the auction is not
measuring how much people want the ride. It is measuring what they have. Time,
for all its faults, is distributed far more equally than money.

**It can make the free queue worse on purpose.** This is the one that should
worry you most. A park's revenue from priority passes depends on the standard
queue being unpleasant. There is a commercial incentive to *not* fix the wait,
and once a system earns money from congestion it has acquired a reason to
preserve it. Watch for this: it is the strongest general argument against
paid-priority schemes, and it applies to airport security, motorway lanes and
customer support alike.

**It changes what kind of thing the good is.** Some queues are for goods we have
decided should not be allocated by price at all — organ transplants, emergency
care, a place in a public school. Introducing payment into those does not just
reallocate them; it reclassifies them. The final week is about that.

## The measurement everyone skips

Here is the empirical question almost no public debate about fast lanes bothers
to ask, and the simulation below is built to answer it: **what actually happens
to the standard queue?**

There are two cases, and they are opposites.

If the paid lane uses *spare* capacity — a counter that was idle — then everyone
is better off and the objection is purely about symbolism.

If the paid lane uses *the same servers* — and it usually does — then every
skip is somebody else's delay. The standard queue gets strictly worse, and the
distribution of the harm is the thing to look at, because the mean barely moves
while the tail lengthens sharply. Move the slider and watch which graph reacts.

Which case you are in is an empirical fact about the system, not a matter of
opinion, and it is astonishing how rarely anyone checks before arguing.
