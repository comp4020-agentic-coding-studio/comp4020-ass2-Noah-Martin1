A restaurant with no reservations has a queue on the pavement. The same
restaurant with a booking system has no queue at all.

It has not become faster. It seats the same number of people in the same
evening. What has changed is that the waiting now happens at home, four days
earlier, distributed across everyone who wanted a table — including the people
who never got one and therefore never appeared in any statistic the restaurant
keeps.

**The wait did not disappear. It moved somewhere nobody measures.** Hold onto
that, because it is the trick every system in this lecture is performing.

## Three ways to move a wait

**Appointments** convert waiting-in-line into waiting-in-calendar. The gain is
real: you spend the interval living your life rather than standing in a
corridor. The costs are two. First, appointment systems are brittle under
variance — one overrunning consultation cascades through the whole afternoon,
which is why your 2:30 GP appointment is at 3:10. Second, they require
commitment in advance, and the people worst served are the ones whose lives are
least predictable, which correlates depressingly well with the people who most
need the service.

**Virtual queues** keep the ordering and delete the standing. Take a ticket, go
away, come back when called. Almost strictly better than a physical line —
except that it silently requires a phone, a number, an address, somewhere to
wait, and the literacy to work the system. The queue has been made invisible,
and so have the people who cannot join it.

**Reservations with deposits** are the paid-priority mechanism of week 8 wearing
a different coat: allocation by willingness to commit money in advance. Every
argument from last week applies unchanged.

## The measurement trap

This is the practical point, and it is why the lecture sits here rather than in
Act I.

When a service moves from a physical queue to an appointment system, its
published waiting time almost always improves dramatically. Of course it does:
it is now measuring the interval between arriving and being seen, and the
four-week gap between phoning and arriving is not in that interval.

A health service that reports "average wait 8 minutes" while people wait five
weeks for the appointment is not lying. It is measuring what it defined itself
as measuring, and the definition is the whole story. Week 3 gave you the tool for
catching this: apply L = λW at the boundary you actually care about, not the one
the organisation chose to draw.

## When it is genuinely the right answer

Do not read this as debunking. Moving a wait is often the best available move,
and here is the honest version: waiting is not uniformly costly. An hour at home
is worth far more than an hour in a corridor. A system that converts the second
into the first has created real value even though the clock says nothing
changed.

The failure is not using appointments. It is using appointments and then
believing your own waiting-time statistic.

price, and appointment — and you have seen that each one buys something and
charges somebody. Act III is about what happens when the system you applied them
