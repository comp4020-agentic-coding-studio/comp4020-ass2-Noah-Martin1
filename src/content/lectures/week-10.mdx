## Why boarding is hard

An aisle is a queue with a property none of our earlier systems had: **the
server and the queue occupy the same space.** A passenger stowing a bag is not
merely slow, they are a moving blockage that stops everyone behind them
regardless of how fast those people would have been.

So boarding time is not governed by average passenger speed. It is governed by
how often a stow blocks the aisle and for how long — which is week 2's lesson,
variance rather than load, in its purest form. An aircraft where everyone had
identical bags and identical seats would board in a fraction of the time with no
change whatever to the mean.

## The strategies

**Back to front** is what many airlines use and what intuition endorses. It is
close to the worst common option. Calling rows 30–36 puts every passenger with a
bag to stow into the same five metres of aisle at the same moment. You have
carefully concentrated the interference.

**Random** does better. It sounds absurd and it is well established: scattering
passengers along the aisle means simultaneous stows mostly do not block one
another.

**Outside-in** (window seats, then middles, then aisles — often called WILMA)
does better still, by eliminating the seat shuffle, which is the other blocking
event.

**Steffen** interleaves both dimensions: alternating rows, outside-in, so
adjacent boarders are two rows apart and never in each other's way. In field
trials it roughly halves boarding time against back-to-front.

## So why does nobody use the fast one?

This is the actually interesting question and it is not a technical one.

**It fights the revenue model.** Priority boarding is sold. Week 8's lesson
applies exactly: an operator earning money from congestion has acquired a reason
to preserve it. Steffen ordering is incompatible with "zone 1 boards first"
because zone 1 is a product.

**It separates people travelling together.** Families ignore the sequence, and
Steffen degrades sharply under non-compliance in a way random boarding does not.
A strategy that is optimal only when everyone obeys is not optimal in an airport.

**It is hard to explain at a gate**, in a second language, over a poor PA, to
tired people.

That gap — between the best known algorithm and the deployed one — is what I
came here to teach. The constraint that binds is almost never the one in the
model.

## The general result

Boarding is one instance of the week's real subject: **once queues are connected,
improving one of them can make the system worse.**

Speed up check-in and you deliver a denser stream to security, which is now the
bottleneck; the total journey time is unchanged and you have moved the crowd into
a smaller room. Open a new motorway lane and the merge point moves downstream,
where it may be worse. Braess's paradox is the formal version: adding capacity to
a network can increase everybody's travel time, and it has been observed in real
road networks.

The practical rule: **a bottleneck that is removed is not gone, it has moved.**
Before you optimise a stage, find out what is downstream of it, or you will spend
a budget relocating a queue and call it an improvement.
