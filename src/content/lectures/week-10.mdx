Here it is. It is the best worked example in the course, because every tool from

<Beat n={1} label="Why an aisle is not an ordinary queue">

<Punch note="A passenger stowing a bag is not merely slow. They are a moving blockage that stops everyone behind them, in a corridor nobody can overtake in.">
  The server and the queue occupy the same space.
</Punch>

Everything in Act I assumed a separation that does not hold here. A bank has a
counter and a lobby; the tellers work while the lobby waits, and the lobby's
length does not slow the tellers down. An aisle has no such division. The person
being served *is* standing in the queue, and the queue cannot flow around them.

So boarding time is not governed by average passenger speed. It is governed by
how often a stow blocks the aisle and for how long — which is week 2's lesson,
variance rather than load, in its purest available form. An aircraft where every
passenger had identical bags and identical seats would board in a fraction of
the time with no change whatever to the mean.

There are exactly two blocking events, and it is worth naming them separately
because the four strategies attack them differently.

**The stow.** Somebody stops at their row, lifts a bag, and everybody behind
them stops. Duration: highly variable, and the variance is what costs you.

**The seat shuffle.** Somebody in an aisle seat has to stand up and step out so
that the window passenger behind them can get in. This one is pure
self-inflicted damage: it happens only because of the *order* people were let
on, and there is a strategy below that eliminates it entirely.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Four strategies">

**Back to front** is what many airlines use and what intuition endorses.

**Random** scatters passengers along the aisle.

**Outside-in** (windows, then middles, then aisles — often called WILMA)
eliminates the seat shuffle, which is the other blocking event.

**Steffen** interleaves both: alternating rows, outside-in, so consecutive
boarders are two rows apart and never in each other's way.

<Predict question="Rank them fastest to slowest. Where does back-to-front land?">

Last. Close to the worst common option, and it is the one most people back.

Calling rows 30 to 36 puts every passenger with a bag to stow into the same five
metres of aisle at the same moment. You have carefully **concentrated** the
interference, which is precisely the thing week 2 told you not to do.

Random beats it, which sounds absurd and is well established. Outside-in beats
random. Steffen roughly halves back-to-front in field trials — and in the
aircraft below it comes in 55% under: <Key>11.9 minutes becomes 5.4</Key>, with
the same passengers, the same bags and the same stow times.

</Predict>

The Steffen result is not only a simulation finding. It was proposed in 2008 and
then **tested with real people** — a mock cabin, volunteers, luggage, timed runs
— and the field experiment broadly confirmed the ordering, with the interleaved
method coming in at around half the time of back-to-front. So the effect
survives contact with actual humans who do not read instructions carefully,
which is more than most operational research manages.

<Beat n={3} label="So why does nobody use the fast one?">

This is the actually interesting question, and it is not a technical one.

**It fights the revenue model.** Priority boarding is a product. Week 8 applies
exactly: an operator earning money from congestion has acquired a reason to
preserve it. Steffen ordering is incompatible with "zone 1 boards first" because
zone 1 is something the airline sells.

**It separates people travelling together.** Families ignore the sequence, and
Steffen degrades sharply under non-compliance in a way random boarding does not.

**It is hard to explain** at a gate, in a second language, over a poor PA, to
tired people.

<Punch note="I ran turnarounds for fifteen years. This is the single most useful thing an operations person can show a student.">
  The constraint that binds is almost never the one in the model.
</Punch>

A strategy that is optimal only when everyone obeys is not optimal in an
airport — which is week 6's warning about self-reported job lengths arriving
again, in a different building, with luggage.

Notice the shape of the comparison, because you will need it in your brief.
Steffen is the best strategy **under compliance** and degrades fast without it.
Random is mediocre under compliance and <Key>degrades not at all, because it has
nothing to degrade from</Key>. Outside-in sits between the two and is what most
airlines that have improved anything have actually adopted, precisely because it
survives families, late arrivals and people who did not hear the announcement.

That is a general design principle and it is worth more than the boarding
result: **rank your options by how they perform when half-followed, not by how
they perform when obeyed.**

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The gate is not one queue, it is a network">

Boarding is the visible queue. It is one node in a small network that has to
complete before the aircraft can move, and the reason the airline cares about
boarding at all is what that network does.

Deplane. Clean. Cater. Fuel. Load the hold. Board. Some of those can run at the
same time; several cannot. Cleaning cannot start until deplaning finishes.
Boarding cannot start until cleaning finishes. Fuelling may or may not overlap
with boarding depending on the operator's rules.

Which produces the first result of network thinking, and it is brutal:
<Key>the turnaround takes as long as the longest chain of things that must
happen in order.</Key> Everything not on that chain has slack, and improving
anything with slack changes nothing at all.

<Predict question="You speed up boarding by four minutes. How much shorter is the turnaround?">

Somewhere between four minutes and nothing, and you cannot tell which without
looking at the rest of the gate.

If boarding is on the critical path, you gained four minutes. If the aircraft
was waiting on a fuel truck that arrives when it arrives, you gained nothing,
and you have spent a change-management budget moving passengers into their seats
earlier so they can sit there longer.

This is why operations people are so unimpressed by improvements to single
steps, and it is the practical content of the **theory of constraints**:
improving a non-bottleneck is not a small win, it is *zero* win. Find the
constraint first, and be prepared for the answer to be something unglamorous
that nobody has a project about.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="Bottlenecks move, they do not disappear">

Now the result that makes networks genuinely dangerous rather than merely
complicated.

<Punch>
  Once queues are connected, improving one of them can make the whole system
  worse.
</Punch>

The mild version first, because it is the one you will actually meet. Speed up
check-in and you deliver a denser stream to security, which is now the
bottleneck. Total journey time is unchanged and you have moved the crowd into a
smaller room with fewer seats and worse air. Somebody will report this as a
success, because check-in's numbers improved and security's numbers belong to a
different manager.

The strong version is **Braess's paradox**, from 1968, and it is worth seeing
once because it is genuinely counter-intuitive: in a road network where drivers
each choose their own fastest route, *adding* a new road can make everybody's
journey longer. Not "can fail to help" — can actively worsen, at equilibrium,
for every single driver. The new road is individually attractive and collectively
destructive, and each driver taking it is behaving perfectly rationally.

The reverse has been observed too. Closing a major road has, in several widely
cited cases, left city-centre traffic no worse and sometimes better, which is
the same mathematics run backwards and is much harder to get funded.

The same structure, in three more buildings:

**Emergency departments.** A department can be working perfectly and still have
a twelve-hour queue, because the wards upstairs are full and patients cannot
leave. The ED is not the bottleneck; **discharge** is. Every intervention aimed
at the visible queue — more triage nurses, a faster front door — pushes harder
against a closed exit. This is well documented and it is the clearest real-world
lesson in the lecture: <Key>the queue you can see is often the symptom of a
constraint somewhere you cannot.</Key>

**Networks.** Adding buffer memory to routers to stop packets being dropped
produced *bufferbloat*: enormous queues, enormous latency, and no more
throughput, because the signal that told senders to slow down was the dropped
packet. The fix for congestion turned out to require keeping the queue short on
purpose.

**Any pipeline of teams.** A development team that doubles its output delivers
twice as much work to a review queue that did not change, and the lead time gets
worse. Week 3's arithmetic predicts it exactly.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="The general result">

Look at the pink bars in the cabin again rather than the clock. Every strategy
loads the same bags into the same lockers, so the total stowing is identical —
and yet the worst row goes from causing eleven and a half minutes of other
people's waiting to causing one. <Key>Back-to-front does not create interference.
It arranges for the interference to happen where the queue is standing.</Key>

That sentence is the whole of Act III's first half, and it generalises past
aircraft: in a connected system, **where** a delay lands matters as much as how
big it is, because a delay that lands on a bottleneck propagates and a delay
that lands on slack evaporates.

<Predict question="Your bottleneck is gone. Where is it?">

Downstream, and probably somewhere with less floor space.

**A bottleneck that is removed has not disappeared — it has moved.** Before you
optimise a stage, find out what is after it, or you will spend a budget
relocating a queue and report it as an improvement.

And the second-order version, which is the one that catches experienced people:
the new bottleneck may be somewhere with *worse* properties than the old one —
less buffer, less staff flexibility, higher variance, or a different department's
budget. Moving a queue from a place designed to hold a queue to a place that was
not is a real way to make a system worse while improving every number you were
watching.

</Predict>
