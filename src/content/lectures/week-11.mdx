Every model in this course so far has treated the things in the queue as jobs:
they arrive, they wait however long they are made to, they leave.

Print jobs behave like this. People do not.

<Beat n={1} label="Three things jobs never do">

**Balking.** Seeing the queue and not joining. This is why a busy shop's
measured demand is not its actual demand, and why a system under strain can look
like it is coping — it is turning people away before they enter the statistics.

Balking has a property that makes it uniquely dangerous to measure: it is
**invisible from inside the system**. A queue with heavy balking looks exactly
like a queue with low demand. Both show short lines, low utilisation and happy
numbers, and only one of them has a problem. The only way to tell them apart is
to count from outside — the footfall past the door against the transactions at
the till — which almost nobody does.

**Reneging.** Joining and then leaving. In a call centre, abandonment is the
single most informative metric and the one most often reported as a cost rather
than read as a signal.

Reneging is also the thing that **stabilises** an overloaded queue, and this is
worth sitting with because it is deeply counter-intuitive. A system with λ above
its capacity should grow without limit; week 2's table has no row for ρ > 1
because there is no steady state there. Real overloaded systems do not grow
without limit. They reach an equilibrium — and the equilibrium is set by how
long people will tolerate before they give up. <Key>The length of an overloaded
queue is a measurement of its customers' patience, not of its capacity.</Key>

**Gaming.** Finding the rule and optimising against it. Week 6 warned about
this: any rule sorting on self-reported information creates an incentive to
misreport.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="The loop">

Each of those makes the queue a feedback system: its state changes people's
behaviour, which changes its state.

<Predict question="You install an accurate live wait estimate. What happens to its accuracy?">

It destroys itself.

Announce nine minutes and some people hang up who would have stayed. The queue
shortens. The wait falls. The number you announced is now wrong — **because you
announced it**. The floor below promises 4.6 minutes to callers who then wait
3.6, and the people who heard it and left are the entire reason it was wrong.

This is not a defect to engineer away. It is what it means to publish
information about a system to that system's own participants. <Key>The estimate
is only ever correct for the counterfactual world in which it was not
shown.</Key>

</Predict>

<Beat n={3} label="Counting the people who left">

The band below the floor in that scene is drawn separately for a reason. Those
callers are not in the building, nobody in it can see them, and in most
organisations nobody measures them either.

This is the point in the course where the standard toolkit runs out. Erlang's
formulas from week 1 assume callers wait as long as it takes. The extension that
does not — usually called **Erlang A**, for *abandonment* — adds one parameter:
a patience distribution. And adding that one parameter changes what every other
number means.

Three consequences, and they are the whole reason a call centre is a different
animal from a bank.

**Abandonment is a better health metric than wait time.** The wait of the people
who were answered is a statistic about survivors. Where patience is short it can
look excellent in a system that is failing, because everybody who would have
produced a long wait has already gone.

**The two obvious levers move different things.** More agents cuts both the wait
and the abandonment. A longer hold message, a better queue position
announcement, music people do not hate — these move *patience*, which cuts
abandonment while making the measured wait **longer**, because more of the
people who would have hung up are now still there to be counted.

<Punch note="Which is why a service-level report should always carry the abandonment rate next to the wait, and why a report carrying only one of them is not interpretable.">
  Improve patience and your waiting-time statistic gets worse.
</Punch>

**The people who left are the most informative population in the system, and
the least likely to be in the data.** Every organisation knows what its served
customers experienced. Almost none knows what the balked ones would have wanted,
because they never became a record.

Which is week 3's boundary argument arriving for the last time. The box was
drawn around the people who were served, and the people who matter most are
outside it.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="The cheapest intervention in the course">

Perceived waiting is not measured waiting — week 4 established the propositions,
and this is where they get used.

An unexplained ten-minute wait is worse than an explained twenty. Progress
information, a position in the queue, and a visible reason all reduce
dissatisfaction with no change whatever to the clock.

That is genuinely the cheapest thing in this entire course, and it improves not
one metric from Act I.

The floor above is the harder version of the same trick, because it *does* move
a metric. At five agents, announcing the wait takes the reported figure from
nine minutes to three and a half — a 60% improvement that any operations
dashboard would celebrate. <Key>The number of callers who failed to get help is
identical: 38%, before and after.</Key> Sixty an hour got through either way.

<Punch note="Week 8's warning again: watch for the operator whose incentive is to manage the perception rather than the wait.">
  Which is exactly why it can be abused. A system that makes waiting feel
  shorter has removed its own pressure to make waiting be shorter.
</Punch>

<Beat n={5} label="What to announce, and what not to">

Given that the announcement changes the queue, what should it say? This is a
real design decision with a real literature, and three options behave
differently.

**Say nothing.** The default, and the worst of the three for everything except
the reported wait. Uncertainty is the most expensive feature of a wait, and
saying nothing maximises it.

**Announce a position.** "You are ninth in the queue." Honest, verifiable, and it
degrades gracefully — a position is a fact, and it cannot be wrong in the way a
time can. Its weakness is that it is uninterpretable: ninth means nothing unless
you know how fast the queue moves.

**Announce a time.** Most useful, most informative, and the one that eats itself.
It is also the one that can be *wrong*, and being wrong here is expensive: a
caller told four minutes who waits nine is angrier than a caller told nothing
who waits nine. The whole benefit of certainty is lost the moment the number
stops being credible.

Which produces the standard practical advice, and it is worth knowing why it
works: <Key>announce conservatively.</Key> Quote a number at the upper end of
what you expect, and most people beat it. The cost is that a few more people
balk at the higher figure. The benefit is that the announcement stays credible,
and credibility is the asset that makes every future announcement work.

<Predict question="Why can a queue not simply announce its true expected wait?">

Because there is no such thing to announce, once you are announcing.

The true expected wait *given that you announce nine minutes* is not nine
minutes — it is whatever results after the people who hear nine minutes leave.
Which is four. But if you announce four, fewer leave, and the real wait rises
above four. The honest number is a **fixed point** of a loop, not a measurement,
and it depends on how your own customers react to being told.

You can in principle solve for it. Almost nobody does, and the ones who do
discover that the fixed point moves when the population changes, which it does
constantly.

<Key>A published estimate is not a description of the system. It is an
intervention in it.</Key>

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Gaming, properly">

The third behaviour deserves its own beat, because it is the one that turns a
queue from a system into a contest.

**Ticket scalping is a queueing attack.** A first-come-first-served allocation
can be defeated by being better at coming first, and software is better at
coming first than people are. Automated purchasing against a major onsale is a
straightforward engineering problem, and the resale price that follows is a
precise measurement of <Key>how much value the original rule was leaving on the
table</Key>. The queue was an auction all along — week 8 — and the bots simply
turned up with better bidding equipment.

Legislation has been tried: the United States banned the circumvention of
ticket-purchase controls in 2016. Enforcement is the difficulty, as it always is
when the attack is indistinguishable from an enthusiastic customer.

**And the same structure, everywhere else.** Sneaker drops answered with raffles,
because a raffle is un-gameable in a way a queue is not. University enrolment
systems at 9am on registration day. Restaurant booking platforms scraped by
resellers. Vaccine appointment scripts in 2021, written by people helping their
parents, which is gaming with entirely sympathetic motives and identical
distributional effects.

Three responses exist, and the choice between them is a design decision you may
have to make.

**Harden the rule.** Rate limits, verification, waiting rooms. An arms race, and
the defender pays more than the attacker.

**Randomise.** A lottery cannot be won by arriving faster. It gives up the
fairness claim of arrival order and buys immunity to speed, which is often a
good trade when the good is scarce and the demand is a spike.

**Price it.** Week 8. Let the willingness to pay be expressed in money rather
than in bot infrastructure, and capture the value instead of burning it.

<Punch>
  Prefer rules that are robust when gamed over rules that are optimal when
  obeyed.
</Punch>

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} label="What this does to design">

Once you accept feedback, a queue stops being something you specify and becomes
something you steer.

A rule that is optimal against a fixed population will be met by a population
that adapts to it, and the second-round behaviour is frequently the whole story:
the express lane fills with people splitting their baskets, the priority
category fills with people who learned the magic words, the fast lane fills up
and stops being fast.

You cannot design this out. You can only design knowing it happens — and your
brief needs a paragraph on what your design does when people lie to it.

Three questions to put to your own design before Friday:

1. **What does somebody gain by misreporting, and how would you know?**
2. **Who can see your queue, and what do they do when they see it?**
3. **Who leaves, and would you find out?**

One week left. Everything so far has been about how queues behave. The last
lecture asks what a queue should be *for*.

</Beat>
