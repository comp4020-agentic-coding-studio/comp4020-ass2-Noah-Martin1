Every model in this course so far has treated the things in the queue as jobs:
they arrive, they wait however long they are made to, they leave.

Print jobs behave like this. People do not.

<Beat n={1} label="Three things jobs never do">

**Balking.** Seeing the queue and not joining. This is why a busy shop's
measured demand is not its actual demand, and why a system under strain can look
like it is coping — it is turning people away before they enter the statistics.

**Reneging.** Joining and then leaving. In a call centre, abandonment is the
single most informative metric and the one most often reported as a cost rather
than read as a signal.

**Gaming.** Finding the rule and optimising against it. Week 6 warned about
this: any rule sorting on self-reported information creates an incentive to
misreport. Ticket scalping is a queueing attack — bots defeat a
first-come-first-served allocation by being better at coming first, and the
resale price measures exactly how much value the original rule was leaving on
the table.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="The loop">

Each of those makes the queue a feedback system: its state changes people's
behaviour, which changes its state.

<Predict question="You install an accurate live wait estimate. What happens to its accuracy?">

It destroys itself.

Display forty minutes and some people leave who would have stayed. The queue
shortens. The wait falls. The number you displayed is now wrong — **because you
displayed it**.

This is not a defect to engineer away. It is what it means to publish
information about a system to that system's own participants. The estimate is
only ever correct for the counterfactual world in which it was not shown.

</Predict>

<Beat n={3} label="The cheapest intervention in the course">

Perceived waiting is not measured waiting.

An unexplained ten-minute wait is worse than an explained twenty. Progress
information, a position in the queue, and a visible reason all reduce
dissatisfaction with no change whatever to the clock.

That is genuinely the cheapest thing in this entire course, and it improves not
one metric from Act I.

<Punch note="Week 8's warning again: watch for the operator whose incentive is to manage the perception rather than the wait.">
  Which is exactly why it can be abused. A system that makes waiting feel
  shorter has removed its own pressure to make waiting be shorter.
</Punch>

Pleasant queues are not automatically good queues.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="What this does to design">

Once you accept feedback, a queue stops being something you specify and becomes
something you steer.

A rule that is optimal against a fixed population will be met by a population
that adapts to it, and the second-round behaviour is frequently the whole story:
the express lane fills with people splitting their baskets, the priority
category fills with people who learned the magic words.

<Punch>
  Prefer rules that are robust when gamed over rules that are optimal when
  obeyed.
</Punch>

You cannot design this out. You can only design knowing it happens — and your
brief needs a paragraph on what your design does when people lie to it.

One week left. Everything so far has been about how queues behave. The last
lecture asks what a queue should be *for*.

</Beat>
