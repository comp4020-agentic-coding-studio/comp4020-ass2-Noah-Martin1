Every model so far has treated the things in the queue as jobs: they arrive, they
wait however long they are made to, they leave. Print jobs behave like this.
People do not.

## Three things jobs never do

**Balking.** Seeing the queue and not joining. This is why a busy shop's measured
demand is not its actual demand, and why a system under strain can look like it
is coping — it is turning people away before they enter the statistics.

**Reneging.** Joining and then leaving. In a call centre, abandonment is the
single most informative metric and the one most often reported as a cost rather
than read as a signal.

**Gaming.** Discovering the rule and optimising against it. Week 6 warned about
this: any rule that sorts on self-reported information creates an incentive to
misreport. Ticket scalping is a queueing attack — bots defeat a
first-come-first-served allocation by being better at coming first, and the
resale price is a measure of how much value the original rule was leaving on the
table.

Each of these makes the queue a **feedback system**: its state changes people's
behaviour, which changes its state.

## Telling people the wait

Now the counter-intuitive part, and the most practically useful finding in the
week.

Display an accurate estimated wait and some people leave who would otherwise have
stayed. The queue shortens. The wait for those remaining falls. The estimate you
displayed is now wrong — because you displayed it.

This is not a defect to be engineered away; it is what it means to publish
information about a system to the system's own participants. It has three real
consequences.

**Accurate estimates are self-defeating in a specific direction.** They are
correct only for the counterfactual in which they were not shown.

**Perceived waiting is not measured waiting.** An unexplained ten-minute wait is
worse than an explained twenty. Progress information, position in queue, and a
visible reason all reduce dissatisfaction with no change to the clock. This is
the cheapest intervention in the entire course and it improves no metric that
appears in Act I.

**Which means it can be abused.** A system that makes waiting feel shorter has
removed its own pressure to make waiting *be* shorter. Pleasant queues are not
automatically good queues, and week 8's warning applies again: watch for the
operator whose incentive is to manage the perception rather than the wait.

## The design consequence

Once you accept feedback, the queue stops being something you can specify in
advance and becomes something you steer. A rule that is optimal against a fixed
population will be met by a population that adapts to it, and the second-round
behaviour is frequently the whole story — the express lane fills with people
splitting their baskets, the priority category fills with people who learned the
magic words.

You cannot design this out. You can only design knowing it happens, which mostly
means preferring rules that are robust when gamed over rules that are optimal
when obeyed.

One week left. Everything so far has been about how queues behave. The last
lecture asks the question the whole course has been circling: what should a queue
be *for*?
