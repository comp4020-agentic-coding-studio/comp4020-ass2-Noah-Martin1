Almost every queue you have met this semester was optimising something without
saying so. This one had to say so, in public, and argue about it for years.

## The list

There are more people needing a kidney than there are kidneys. No amount of
process improvement fixes that; it is not a queueing problem in the sense of the
first nine weeks, because there is no throughput to be found. The only question
is order, and order here decides who lives.

Every rule you have learned has been proposed for it, seriously, by serious
people.

**First come, first served** — allocate by time on the list. Transparent,
ungameable, requires no judgement about whose life is worth more. It also wastes
organs: a kidney given to a 70-year-old yields far fewer life-years than the same
kidney given to a 25-year-old, and under pure FCFS that difference is invisible
by design.

**Shortest job first**, in this context, is allocation by expected benefit —
give the organ where it produces the most life-years. Maximises the good done
per organ. It also systematically deprioritises the old, the diabetic and the
already-sick, which is to say it allocates least to those who are worst off.

**Triage** — allocate by urgency, sickest first. Matches the medical instinct
and our week 7 consent. It is also the least efficient rule available: the
sickest patients have the worst outcomes, so this maximises organs used and
minimises life-years produced.

**Price.** Illegal almost everywhere. Iran is the notable exception and the
literature on it is genuinely worth reading rather than dismissing. Notice that
the objection here is not efficiency — a market would clear — but that we have
decided this is not a thing that should be allocated by ability to pay at all.

The actual US system, after two decades of public argument, is a weighted
composite that pleases nobody entirely, revised repeatedly, with the weights
published. That is not a failure to find the right answer. **It is what choosing
looks like when you are forced to do it in the open.**

## The four objectives

Every queue in this course optimises for some mixture of four things, and they
genuinely conflict:

- **the mean** — total waiting, minimised
- **the tail** — the worst case, bounded
- **fairness** — however you have defined it, and you must define it
- **revenue** — value captured by the operator

Week 6 showed mean and tail pulling against each other. Week 8 showed revenue
pulling against both. Week 7 showed that fairness is not one thing. There is no
setting that maximises all four, which means **every queue has already made this
trade** — including, especially, the ones that have never discussed it.

## The thesis, finally

> There is no neutral queue.

A system that has not chosen has not escaped the choice. It has inherited one,
usually from whoever laid out the room, and it is enforcing that choice on
everybody who walks in, without the courtesy of having said what it is.

This is why the course spent four weeks on measurement before touching a single
ordering rule. You cannot argue about what a queue should optimise until you can
say what it currently does, in numbers, and name who is paying. That is the whole
method: **observe, model, experiment, cost, decide** — and then say out loud what
you decided.

## What you can do now that you could not in February

You can walk into a building, watch a line for ninety seconds, and state the rule
it is running. You can measure it from the outside with a stopwatch. You can put
a number on what it costs and say who is holding the bill. You can propose a
different rule and predict, quantitatively, who wins and who loses. And you can
tell the difference between a queue that is badly designed and a queue that is
designed against you.

Your design brief is due Friday. The final page asks you to state what your queue
optimises for. Everything since week 1 has been preparation for being able to
finish that sentence honestly.
