Almost every queue you have met this semester was optimising something without
saying so.

This one had to say so, in public, and argue about it for two decades.

<Beat n={1} label="A queue with no throughput to find">

There are more people needing a kidney than there are kidneys.

No amount of process improvement fixes that. It is not a queueing problem in the
sense of the first nine weeks, because there is no efficiency left to extract —
every organ gets used. <Key>The only question is order, and here order decides
who lives.</Key>

Every rule you have learned has been seriously proposed for it. So the unit
below runs all five, on the same list, and you can watch each one pick.

</Beat>

<Beat n={2} label="Run your five rules on it">

**First come, first served** — allocate by time on the list. Transparent,
ungameable, requires no judgement about whose life is worth more. It also wastes
organs: a kidney given to a 70-year-old yields far fewer life-years than the
same kidney given to a 25-year-old, and under pure FCFS that difference is
invisible by design.

those who are worst off.

**Triage** — sickest first. Matches the medical instinct and week 7's consent.
It is also the *least* efficient rule available, because the sickest patients
have the worst outcomes: it maximises organs used and minimises life-years
produced.

<Predict question="So which one did the United States pick?">

None of them, and that is the answer.

After two decades of public argument it runs a **weighted composite** that
pleases nobody entirely, gets revised repeatedly, and publishes its weights.

That is not a failure to find the right answer. It is what choosing looks like
when you are forced to do it in the open, with everybody watching, and cannot
retreat into the pretence that the current rule is just how things are done.

The fifth setting below is that composite: four points of waiting time against
four of expected benefit against two of urgency. <Key>It is second best at
everything, and that is not a compromise — it is the design.</Key>

</Predict>

<Beat n={3} label="The four objectives, and why you cannot have them all">

The readout above is the matrix below, live. Life-years an organ is efficiency;
W is the mean; the slowest tenth is the tail; organs reaching the over-sixties
is one concrete reading of fairness.

Read across the five settings and the shape is unmistakable. Sorting by benefit
produces nearly a third more life-years than arrival order — 12.6 against 9.7 —
and cuts the share of organs reaching the over-sixties from 43% to 24%.

Then look at who is *in* the room. Under arrival order the over-sixties are 42%
of the list. Under most-life-years they are <Key>83% of it</Key> — because they
are the people it never picks, so they stay, and they keep getting older while
they do. The rule does not merely under-serve them. It fills the unit with them
and then declines them faster.

Sickest-first has the most medical consent of any rule here and is the least
efficient on offer, at 9.0, with a slowest tenth of 12.0 years against arrival
order's 4.8. That is week 7's starved bottom category exactly, except that here
the bottom category is whoever is coping best — which is why, if you leave the
scene running on that setting, the pink mark lands on somebody in their thirties
who has been on the list for decades.

<ObjectiveMatrix />

Week 6 showed mean and worst-case pulling against each other. Week 8 showed
revenue pulling against both. Week 7 showed that fairness is not even one thing.

There is no setting that maximises all four. Which means **every queue has
already made this trade** — including, especially, the ones that have never
discussed it.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} label="One last trap, and one last conservation law">

Two things in that readout are worth stopping on, because both are traps you
were taught to spot.

**The wait is measured over everybody who left the list, not everybody who got
an organ.** Measure only the transplanted and sorting by benefit appears to
*shorten* the queue: 0.9 years, against the 2.2 the readout reports. It does,
for the people it picks. The people it never picks sit there until they die and
leave the statistic without ever having entered it. <Key>That is week 9's
boundary, drawn one last time around the people a rule excludes.</Key>

Under arrival order the same two figures are 2.9 and 2.7 — no gap worth
mentioning, because that rule is not selecting on anything. So the gap between
those two numbers is not noise. It *is* the sorting, measured.

**And the last number barely moves.** Thirty-five to thirty-eight per cent
leave without an organ under every single rule.

<Predict question="Five rules, five different orderings, and the same share die. Why?">

Because ordering is conservative, and this is week 8's theorem with the stakes
changed.

More people join than there are organs. Nobody is served faster, no organ goes
unused, and the surplus has to leave the list some other way — so the share who
never get one is fixed by arithmetic before any rule is chosen. Reordering can
only decide *which* people are in it.

<Punch note="Drag it from three to six and the share who die goes from about half to about one in nine, under whichever rule is selected. Nothing else in the scene comes close.">
  The only control in the room that changes how many people die is the supply.
</Punch>

Which is the honest end of the course. Every ordering rule is a way of deciding
who bears a shortage, and none of them is a way of ending one. If your design
brief can increase capacity, that dominates every clever rule in here.

</Predict>

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} label="What the real system actually does">

The scene above runs five pure rules because pure rules are what a course can
teach. No real allocation system runs one, and the US kidney system is worth
describing in outline because it is the most argued-over queue on earth and
every fix in it is a response to a failure this course has already covered.

It is a points system. Candidates accumulate points, and the components are
recognisable:

**Waiting time** — week 5, and the fix applied to it is the most instructive
detail in the whole system. The clock used to start when you joined the list,
which quietly rewarded whoever had a doctor who referred them early. It now runs
from the start of dialysis, which is a fact about the patient rather than about
their access to a referral. <Key>That single change is week 5's lecture in
practice: arrival order is an allocation that already happened somewhere
else.</Key>

**Sensitisation** — a measure of how hard a candidate is to match. Points rise
steeply for the hardest-to-match, because otherwise they never get an offer at
all. This is an ageing rule, in exactly week 6's sense, applied to a starvation
problem the pure rules produce.

**Paediatric status**, and priority for prior living donors: explicit ethical
commitments, written into the arithmetic, and defensible as such.

**Longevity matching** — the most contested part, introduced in 2014. Roughly
the best fifth of kidneys are offered first to roughly the fifth of candidates
with the best expected post-transplant survival. That is week 6's rule, limited
on purpose to a fraction of the supply rather than applied to all of it.

And in 2021 the geography changed: fixed regional boundaries were replaced with
distance-based circles around the donor hospital, because a boundary is a
discontinuity and people on the wrong side of one were waiting years longer for
no defensible reason.

<Punch note="Which is what a designed queue looks like after thirty years of public argument: not one rule, but five, each limited so that no single objective can run away with the supply.">
  Every component in that list is a correction to a failure you have already
  met in this course.
</Punch>

Notice the design move that makes it work, because it is the one you should
steal. Longevity matching is not applied to every organ. It is applied to a
**capped fraction** of them. <Key>Bounding how much of the supply any one
objective may claim is how a system gets most of the efficiency without any of
the starvation</Key> — and it is available to you in almost any design, at the
cost of being explicit about the fraction.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} label="Other places society had to say it out loud">

Organ allocation is unusual only in how long the argument has been running. Once
you can see the shape, you can see the same public reckoning in several places
within living memory.

**Vaccine prioritisation, 2020–21.** Every country published an ordering, in
advance, in a document anybody could read: by age, by clinical risk, by
occupation. The arguments were ferocious and they were arguments about
*objectives* — lives saved against years saved against keeping hospitals
staffed — which are precisely the columns of the matrix above. It is the largest
triage exercise ever conducted in public.

**ICU protocols in a pandemic.** Several health systems wrote down, in advance,
how ventilators would be allocated if there were not enough. Publishing it was
agonising and correct: a rule written in advance can be argued with, and a rule
improvised at the bedside cannot.

**School places.** Catchment, sibling priority, lottery, or a mixture. A lottery
is chosen specifically because it cannot be gamed by moving house — it trades
away a fairness claim to buy immunity from a form of gaming, which is week 11's
trade with children attached.

**Social housing.** A points system, published, with the weights visible. The
same structure as the transplant list and the same objections.

What those four have in common is not that they got the answer right. It is that
<Key>they were made to publish the rule</Key> — and publishing turns out to be
the intervention, because a published rule can be objected to by the people it
sorts.

Set that against everything else in this course. The café's layout, the
supermarket's express lane, the call centre's hold message, the airline's
boarding zones: every one of those is an allocation rule affecting thousands of
people a day, and not one of them is written down anywhere the people in the
queue can read it.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} label="The thesis, finally">

<Punch note="It has inherited one, usually from whoever laid out the room, and it is enforcing that choice on everybody who walks in without the courtesy of having said what it is.">
  There is no neutral queue. A system that has not chosen has not escaped the
  choice.
</Punch>

This is why the course spent four weeks on measurement before touching a single
ordering rule. You cannot argue about what a queue should optimise until you can
say what it currently does, in numbers, and name who is paying.

That is the whole method: **observe, model, experiment, cost, decide** — and
then say out loud what you decided. The board on the wall of that unit is doing
the only genuinely admirable thing available to it: <Key>it has written the rule
down where the people it sorts can read it.</Key>

</Beat>
