I spent six years modelling patient flow for a hospital network, on the
assumption that the waiting was a capacity problem. It was not. The department I
was assigned to had enough staff, enough beds and enough hours; what it did not
have was agreement about who should be seen first. We could have cut the median
wait by a third in a fortnight. It took two years, because the change everybody
resisted was not the arithmetic, it was the ordering rule.

That is the experience this course is built out of. I am not very interested in
teaching you to compute the mean wait of an M/M/1 queue, though you will learn
to, in week 3, and it takes about forty minutes. I am interested in the fact
that almost every queue you meet is running a rule nobody in the building can
name, that the rule is doing distributive work, and that the people it costs the
most are usually the least able to complain about it.

My own research is on queues run by governments: passport offices, visa
processing, social security, public housing registers. They are the ones where
the wait is longest, the people waiting have the fewest alternatives, and the
cost is least often written down anywhere.

I convene this course, give most of the lectures, and mark the final design
brief. If you want to argue with something I said in a lecture, Wednesday
afternoon is the right time and I will enjoy it.
