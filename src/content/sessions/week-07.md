Read the week 7 notes, including Priya's section. Nothing else — preparation

## In the lab

**The sort.** Forty case cards, adapted from published triage training material.
Ninety seconds each, alone, no discussion. Assign a category 1 to 5. You will
feel rushed. That is the working condition, not a flaw in the exercise.

**The reference.** We reveal the reference categories. Everybody scores
themselves. Expect to be wrong on somewhere between six and twelve, which is
roughly where experienced clinicians sit on unfamiliar cards, so do not read your
score as a verdict on you.

**The interesting part: the pattern.** Errors are not evenly distributed and
yours will not be either. Cross-tabulate the room's mistakes against the card
features — was the patient described as calm, as a frequent presenter, as
distressed, as elderly, as not speaking English as a first language? Nearly every
cohort discovers the same two clusters, and discovers them in their own data
rather than being told about them.

**The consequence.** Then we put the classifier back in the queue. Take your own
error rate from part two and apply it to the priority discipline from the
lecture. A 15% misclassification rate does not degrade the queue by 15%; it
concentrates almost the whole cost on the small group being misclassified
downwards, who then wait under the wrong category's target with the wrong
condition.

## Afterwards

This lab has upset people in previous years, and it is meant to be uncomfortable
rather than gratuitous. If it raises something about clinical work you would like
to talk about away from the room, email me and we will find a time.

The design point carries into the final brief: every priority scheme contains a
classifier, the classifier has an error profile, and the error profile is part of
the design whether or not the designer looked at it.
