## Before the lab

Pick a real system with a rule you think you could beat. Nothing illegal, and
nothing you intend to actually do — this is analysis, not fieldwork.

## In the lab

**Attack.** In groups, take each system and find the exploit. Be specific. "You
could game it" is not an answer; "split a 14-item basket into two 7-item baskets
and use the express lane twice, saving four minutes at a cost of thirty seconds"
is. Every self-reported priority rule has one of these, and half the systems in
the room will turn out to have an exploit that requires no dishonesty at all,
just attention.

**Cost it.** What is the exploit worth per use? What proportion of users would
have to find it before the rule stops working? Some rules survive being widely
known and some collapse; the difference is worth more than the exploit itself.

**Defend.** Now design the counter. Then — and this is the part groups skip —
state what the counter costs the honest users. Verification is friction, and
friction lands on everybody, including the people who were never going to cheat.
A rule hardened against a rare exploit at the price of an extra check for
everyone is frequently a bad trade, and you should be able to say roughly when.

**Present.** Four minutes per group. The room votes on which counter it would
actually accept as a customer, which is a different question from which one is
most effective, and the gap between those two votes is the point of the lab.

## Afterwards

Design briefs are due at the end of week 12. If your design has a rule that
sorts on anything a user tells you, today should have convinced you that you need
a paragraph on what happens when they lie.
