## Before the lab

Bring your design brief in whatever state it is in. It is due Friday; today is
the last chance to find out what is wrong with it while there is still time to
fix it.

## In the lab

**Four minutes each, no slides.** Present the system, the diagnosis, the change,
and who pays. No notes. If you cannot do it in four minutes without notes, the
argument is not finished, and that is useful to discover today rather than in
the marking.

**The steelman round.** This is the format that matters and it is why the lab
runs the way it does. After you present, somebody else in the room — not you —
argues for the alternative you rejected. They argue for it properly, at its
strongest, having had ten minutes to prepare it.

You then respond. The exercise is unpleasant in exactly the right way: most
people discover that the alternative they dismissed in a sentence has a better
case than they had allowed, and they still have three days to deal with it. That
is the single highest-value hour in the semester, measured in marks.

**The last question.** Before you sit down, you answer it out loud: *what does
your queue optimise for?* One sentence. Mean, tail, fairness, revenue, or a
stated mixture with the weights named.

Nobody is allowed to say "all of them". Week 12's lecture is thirty minutes of
why that answer does not exist.

## Afterwards

Briefs due Friday 17:00.

That is the course. You arrived able to stand in a queue and be annoyed by it.
You leave able to say what it is doing, to whom, on whose authority, and what it
would take to change — which is a much more uncomfortable way to go through a
supermarket, and we make no apology for it.
