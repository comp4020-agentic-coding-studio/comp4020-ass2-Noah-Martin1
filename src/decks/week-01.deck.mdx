## Not one decision. Dozens.

- along the window, not doubling back
- one queue, not two
- the person taking orders is not the person making the drink
- a filter coffee waits behind four oat lattes

---

{/* _class: quote */}

Some of it was designed by somebody thinking about throughput.

At least one of it was a shopfitter in 2019 thinking about power outlets.

```notes
This is the line that lands. Pause after it.

One of those decisions is costing about four minutes per customer at peak and
nobody in the building could tell you which one.
```

## Four knobs

Every queue, everywhere:

- **arrivals** — when people turn up
- **service** — how long each takes
- **servers** — how many at once
- **discipline** — *who goes next*

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Almost everything in this course

## is a consequence of turning one knob

```notes
And it is the fourth one. Nobody has strong feelings about inter-arrival
variance. Everybody has strong feelings about queue-jumping.
```

---

## The claim

> A queue is not a thing that happens to people.
>
> It is a rule, applied to people, by someone, usually invisibly.

---

## Which means

Deciding **who goes first** is deciding **whose time is spent**.

Time is the one thing everybody in the line is paying with, whether they agreed
to or not.

So the rule is moving cost from one group of people to another —

and almost never saying so.

## This is not a queueing theory course

You will have the core of the mathematics by week 3.

It takes about forty minutes.

The mathematics answers **how long**.

It does not answer **who**.

---

## The rule we will hold you to

**Name the rule before you judge it.**

Most complaint about waiting turns out to be complaint about a rule the
complainer has never articulated.

Once you can state it, you can ask the useful question: what would it cost to
change?
