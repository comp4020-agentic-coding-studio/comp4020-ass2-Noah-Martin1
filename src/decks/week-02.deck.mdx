## What actually makes a queue

A **gap** — work arrives while the server is still busy with something that ran
long.

Bad luck accumulates. Good luck does not.

```notes
Ten seconds lost to a long order is gone for ever. Ten seconds of empty window
is not banked anywhere. This asymmetry is the entire mechanism and it is worth
saying twice.
```

---

## Measuring raggedness

**c** = standard deviation ÷ mean, on the gaps between arrivals

- **c = 0** — a metronome
- **c = 1** — memoryless. People arriving without coordinating
- **c > 1** — bursty. A lecture ending

## One equation

# W ≈ V × U × T

**V** — variability · **U** — ρ/(1−ρ) · **T** — service time

```notes
Kingman, 1961. Called the VUT equation in operations. Do the drive-through on
the board: V=1, U=11.5, T=55s → 633 seconds → eleven minutes. Then set V=0 and
watch the whole thing collapse to zero. That is lane A.
```

---

## Three systems, one equation

**Motorways** — flow breaks down and the bottleneck then discharges *slower*
than before it jammed. Ramp metering is a V-and-U device.

**Hospital beds** — the 85% occupancy target is this table, with a bed as the
server.

**Servers** — p99 latency takes off long before the mean. Autoscaling at 70%
CPU is buying the gap.

---

## Two sources — and one you make yourself

**Arrivals** — mostly cannot remove. Can reshape *(week 9)*.

**Service** — often can reduce. Short menus, split stations, separate desks.

**Batching** — variability you manufacture, to save a setup cost, and bill to
the queue.

```notes
Ask for batches in their own life. Weekly deploys, the ward round, the 10-to-the
-hour lecture release, the coffee order for the office. Every one is a setup
cost saved by somebody who is not in the queue.
```
