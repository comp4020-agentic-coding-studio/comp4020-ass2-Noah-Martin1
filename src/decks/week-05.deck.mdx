## Around 1970

Banks replaced **one queue per teller** with **one queue for all tellers**.

Customers hated it.

It looked longer. It *was* longer.

```notes
Thirty in one line instead of six lines of five — and the thirty are the same
thirty. They needed posters to explain it.
```

## But the gain shrinks with size

Staffing ≈ **work + √work**

- 4 tellers' work → ~2 of safety → **50% overhead**
- 100 tellers' work → ~10 of safety → **10% overhead**

```notes
So pooling pays most where the pools are smallest. Merging four single-server
lines is transformative. Merging four teams of ten is worth having and is not
the same animal. Ask them where the single-server queues are in their own
organisation — that is where the damage is.
```

---

## Four reasons not to pool

- generalists may be **slower** than specialists
- pooling costs **travel**
- it **couples** things that were independent
- some partitions are **deliberate** (the express lane, the resus bay)

---

## And the real reason people hated four lines

With four lanes, yours is fastest **one time in four**.

So three-quarters of the time, somebody who arrived after you leaves before you.

And you watch them do it.

```notes
The single line does not reduce that on average — it makes it impossible. That
is worth more than the thirteen minutes, and it shows up in complaint volumes
long before it shows up in waits.
```

## The fairness claim, properly

- needs **no information** about you
- **verifiable from inside** the queue
- **strategy-proof**, except by arriving earlier
- needs **no justification** at the counter
