## The proof is two lines

Longer job ahead of a shorter one? **Swap them.**

Short job saves a lot. Long job loses a little. Total falls.

So the optimal order has no such pair. So it is sorted.

```notes
An exchange argument. No probability, no simulation, no assumptions about
arrivals. It is the only unarguable thing in Act II.
```

## What is that last one doing?

```notes
Let them work it out. It is running shortest-job-first without being told the
job lengths — a job that keeps finishing its slice early is *behaving* like a
short job. It infers from behaviour instead of demanding a declaration. That is
the most transferable idea in the lecture.
```

---

## Two general moves

**Infer, don't ask.** Behaviour cannot be misreported as easily as a form.

**Age the queue.** Priority rises with waiting. Bounds the tail, costs the mean.

---

## The dial

Ageing up → **FCFS**: fair, bounded, slower

Ageing down → **SJF**: fast, with somebody there at closing time

No correct setting. Only a defensible one.

```notes
Defensible means: you can say which percentile you chose to protect and what
the mean cost you paid. They plot this curve in the lab.
```

---

## The prerequisite nobody mentions

You must know the job length **before you run it**.

- **observe a proxy** — basket size, page count
- **predict from history** — fails silently on anyone new
- **ask** — and now you have built an incentive

```notes
Ten items or fewer, gamed by a split basket and a partner. Six yoghurts: one
item or six? That is a priority system being negotiated in a car park.
```
