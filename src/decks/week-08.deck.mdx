## A queue is an auction denominated in time

The people who reach the front are the people willing to bid ninety minutes of
their life.

```notes
So what exactly is good about time as the currency? Not that it is equally
distributed — week 4 settled that.
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

A queue and a price sort the same people

in the same order,

and only one of them sets the money on fire.

```notes
Deadweight loss. When you pay ninety minutes, nobody receives ninety minutes.
When you pay forty dollars, the park receives forty dollars. That is the
economist's case and it is strong — make it properly before attacking it.
```

## Three objections, hardest last

**Wealth, not valuation.** Willingness *and ability*.

**A reason not to fix the queue.** Revenue depends on standby being unpleasant.

**It reclassifies the good.** Organs, emergency care, a school place.

Priority is work-conserving.

Nobody serves faster, nobody goes home,

so the mean **cannot** change.

```notes
Which means an operator reporting that the fast lane did not raise the average
is stating a theorem, not presenting evidence. It was true before they built it.
```

Stockholm is the instructive one. Support was substantially higher after people

## Priority inflation

At 80% uptake: a pass buys **5.6 minutes** instead of **3.0**.

Same price.

The value of a fast lane is not a property of the lane. It is a property of how
few people are in it.

---

## The checklist

1. Spare capacity, or shared?
2. Who is in the slow lane?
3. Where does the revenue go?
4. At what uptake does it stop working — and who is watching?

```notes
Run it live on a proposal from the room. Paid priority for elective surgery
using the same theatres is the one that always comes up, and it fails at
question one.
```
