## Three ways to move a wait

**Appointments** — line into calendar

**Virtual queues** — keep the order, delete the standing

**Deposits** — week 8 wearing a different coat

All three keep arrival order. All three change **where you stand**.

---

## The diary has its own mathematics

Two costs you are trading:

**patients waiting** ⟷ **the doctor idle**

No schedule eliminates both, and the size of the trade is set by week 2's
coefficient of variation.

```notes
Bailey, 1952: book two patients at the start of the session, then one per mean
consultation. The double booking exists so the doctor is never idle at the one
moment when idleness is guaranteed.
```

---

## Why your 2:30 is at 3:10

Delay **accumulates** through a session and nothing pushes it back.

So 9am runs on time and 4:30 does not. Always. Everywhere.

## A clinic runs 40 minutes late every afternoon.

## Cheapest fix?

```notes
Two empty slots in the middle. Not faster consultations — the mean is not the
problem, the variance is. It costs one appointment of capacity and is nearly
always refused, because a gap in the diary is visible to management and a
forty-minute delay is visible only to patients.
```

---

## Virtual queues: nearly a free win

Ordering preserved. Hours returned. Best benefit-to-cost ratio in the course.

**And** it has an eligibility criterion nobody wrote down: a phone, charge,
data, a number, somewhere to wait, confidence with the interface.

A physical queue excludes nobody who can stand in it.

## The measurement trap

Walk-in: published wait **90 minutes**

Booked: published wait **8 minutes**

The four weeks before the appointment are outside the definition.

```notes
Nobody lied. The figure measures exactly what the service defined it to
measure. That is what makes it so hard to argue with and so common.
```

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## An 18-week maximum, published.

## What shape does the distribution take?

```notes
A pile-up just inside and a cliff just outside. A manager with a patient at 17
weeks and one at 20 has a strong reason to treat the 17 — the other has already
breached and cannot be un-breached.

The patient who has waited longest becomes the patient it is least valuable to
treat. Nobody designed that priority queue.
```

---

{/* _class: quote */}

A published metric

becomes an ordering rule.

---

## The partial defence

Report the **distribution**, or at minimum the **tail** as well as the rate.

"Share seen within 18 weeks" **and** "longest anyone waited".

The second gets worse exactly when the first is being protected.
